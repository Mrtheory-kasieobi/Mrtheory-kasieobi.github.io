Chain Rule and Implicit Differentiation

Calculus I - Chapter 3.5-3.9 (Detailed Notes)

1. The Chain Rule

1.1 Understanding Composite Functions

Composite Function: If \(g\) is a function and \(f\) is a function, the composition of \(f\) and \(g\) is: $$(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$$

Read as "f composed with g" or "f of g of x"

Decomposing Composite Functions: To apply the chain rule, identify:
  • Outside function: The "outer" operation
  • Inside function: The "inner" function that's plugged in
Example: For \(\sin(x^2)\):
  • Outside: \(\sin(u)\)
  • Inside: \(u = x^2\)

1.2 Statement and Proof of the Chain Rule

Chain Rule: If \(g\) is differentiable at \(a\) and \(f\) is differentiable at \(g(a)\), then \(f \circ g\) is differentiable at \(a\) and: $$(f \circ g)'(a) = f'(g(a)) \cdot g'(a)$$ Or equivalently: \(\frac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\)
Proof of the Chain Rule: We need to show: \(\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(g(a+h)) - f(g(a))}{h} = f'(g(a)) \cdot g'(a)\) Key idea: Multiply and divide by \(g(a+h) - g(a)\): $$\frac{f(g(a+h)) - f(g(a))}{h} = \frac{f(g(a+h)) - f(g(a))}{g(a+h) - g(a)} \cdot \frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h}$$ Provided \(g(a+h) \neq g(a)\), this equals: $$\frac{f(g(a+h)) - f(g(a))}{g(a+h) - g(a)} \cdot \frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h}$$ Now take the limit as \(h \to 0\): First fraction: As \(h \to 0\), \(g(a+h) \to g(a)\) (by continuity of \(g\)).

So \(\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(g(a+h)) - f(g(a))}{g(a+h) - g(a)} = f'(g(a))\)

Second fraction: \(\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h} = g'(a)\)

Therefore:

\((f \circ g)'(a) = f'(g(a)) \cdot g'(a)\)

Technical note: We need to handle the case where \(g(a+h) = g(a)\) for some small \(h\). This can be done by defining an appropriate "error function" that makes the formula work even when \(g(a+h) = g(a)\). ∎

1.3 Applying the Chain Rule

Chain Rule Algorithm:
  1. Identify the outside and inside functions
  2. Differentiate the outside function (treat inside as a single variable)
  3. Multiply by the derivative of the inside function
  4. Simplify if possible
Example 1.1 - Basic Chain Rule: Find \(\frac{d}{dx}[\sin(x^2)]\)

Step 1 - Identify: Outside: \(\sin(u)\), Inside: \(u = x^2\)

Step 2 - Differentiate outside: \(\frac{d}{du}[\sin u] = \cos u\)

Step 3 - Multiply by derivative of inside: \(\cos(x^2) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[x^2] = \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x\)

Step 4 - Write final answer: \(\frac{d}{dx}[\sin(x^2)] = 2x\cos(x^2)\)

Example 1.2 - Nested Chain Rule: Find \(\frac{d}{dx}[e^{\sin(x^2)}]\)

Layer 1 (outside to inside):

Outermost: \(e^u\) → derivative: \(e^u\)

Middle: \(\sin(v)\) → derivative: \(\cos(v)\)

Innermost: \(x^2\) → derivative: \(2x\)

Chain it together:

$$\frac{d}{dx}[e^{\sin(x^2)}] = e^{\sin(x^2)} \cdot \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x = 2xe^{\sin(x^2)}\cos(x^2)$$

Example 1.3 - Chain Rule with Product: Find \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^2 \sin(x^3)]\)

Use product rule: \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\)

Let \(u = x^2\), \(v = \sin(x^3)\)

\(u' = 2x\)

For \(v'\), use chain rule: \(\cos(x^3) \cdot 3x^2\)

Result: \(2x \cdot \sin(x^3) + x^2 \cdot 3x^2 \cos(x^3)\)

= \(2x\sin(x^3) + 3x^4\cos(x^3)\)

2. Implicit Differentiation

2.1 What is Implicit Differentiation?

Implicit Function: An equation of the form \(F(x, y) = 0\) where \(y\) is considered an implicit function of \(x\). Implicit Differentiation: A technique for finding \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) when \(y\) is defined implicitly as a function of \(x\).
When to Use Implicit Differentiation:
  • The equation cannot be easily solved for \(y\)
  • We want to find \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) without explicit formula for \(y\)
  • The equation relates \(x\) and \(y\) in a complex way

2.2 The Method

Implicit Differentiation Formula: If \(F(x, y) = 0\) defines \(y\) as a function of \(x\), then: $$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}$$ provided \(F_y \neq 0\).
Derivation of the Formula: Assume \(y\) is a function of \(x\), so \(y = y(x)\). Differentiate \(F(x, y) = 0\) with respect to \(x\): $$\frac{d}{dx}[F(x, y)] = 0$$ Using the chain rule for multivariable functions: $$\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} \cdot \frac{dx}{dx} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 0$$ Since \(\frac{dx}{dx} = 1\): $$F_x + F_y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 0$$ Solving for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\): $$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}$$ ∎
Example 2.1 - Circle: Find \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) for \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\)

Step 1 - Differentiate both sides:

\(\frac{d}{dx}[x^2 + y^2] = \frac{d}{dx}[25]\)

Step 2 - Apply chain rule to \(y^2\):

\(2x + 2y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 0\)

Step 3 - Solve for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\):

\(2y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = -2x\)

\(\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}\)

Example 2.2 - More Complex: Find \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) for \(x^3 + y^3 = 6xy\) (folium of Descartes)

Step 1 - Differentiate:

\(3x^2 + 3y^2 \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 6y + 6x \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}\)

Step 2 - Collect \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) terms:

\(3y^2 \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} - 6x \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 6y - 3x^2\)

Step 3 - Factor and solve:

\(\frac{dy}{dx}(3y^2 - 6x) = 6y - 3x^2\)

\(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{6y - 3x^2}{3y^2 - 6x} = \frac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x}\)

3. Related Rates (Introduction)

3.1 The Related Rates Method

Related Rates: A technique for finding the rate of change of one quantity in terms of the rate of change of another related quantity.
General Strategy:
  1. Identify variables: Let \(x, y, r, \theta\), etc. represent the quantities
  2. Identify rates: Note which rates are given (\(\frac{dx}{dt}, \frac{dy}{dt}\)) and which you need to find
  3. Write equation relating variables: Use geometry, physics, or given information
  4. Differentiate with respect to time: Apply chain rule to both sides
  5. Substitute and solve: Plug in known values and solve for unknown rate
Example 3.1 - Classic Related Rates: A ladder 10 ft long leans against a wall. If the bottom slides away from the wall at 2 ft/s, how fast is the top sliding down when the bottom is 6 ft from the wall?

Step 1 - Variables: \(x\) = distance from wall to bottom, \(y\) = height of top

Step 2 - Given rate: \(\frac{dx}{dt} = 2\) ft/s (bottom sliding away)

Step 3 - Relationship: By Pythagorean theorem: \(x^2 + y^2 = 100\)

Step 4 - Differentiate: \(2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0\)

Step 5 - Substitute: When \(x = 6\), \(y = 8\)

\(2(6) + 2(8)\frac{dy}{dt} = 0\)

\(\frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{12}{16} = -\frac{3}{4}\)

Answer: The top slides down at \(\frac{3}{4}\) ft/s

3.2 Second Derivative Test

Second Derivative Test for Extrema: Suppose \(f'(c) = 0\) and \(f''(c)\) exists.
  • If \(f''(c) > 0\), then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(c\)
  • If \(f''(c) < 0\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\)
  • If \(f''(c) = 0\), the test is inconclusive
Proof for the case \(f''(c) > 0\) (local minimum): Since \(f''(c) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f'(c+h) - f'(c)}{h} > 0\) and \(f'(c) = 0\): For small \(h > 0\): \(\frac{f'(c+h) - 0}{h} > 0 \Rightarrow f'(c+h) > 0\)

For small \(h < 0\): \(\frac{f'(c+h) - 0}{h} > 0 \Rightarrow f'(c+h) < 0\) So \(f'\) changes from negative to positive at \(c\), meaning \(f\) has a local minimum there. ∎
