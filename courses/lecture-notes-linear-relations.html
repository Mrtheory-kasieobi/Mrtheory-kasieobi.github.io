Lecture 1: Measurement

Mathematics 10 - Chapter 1

1.1 Imperial Measures of Length

The Imperial system measures length in inches, feet, yards, and miles. It is still used in the United States and in a few industries such as construction and aviation, so it is useful to know how the units relate to one another.

A measurement is a number that describes a size, such as a length, an area, or a volume. It always includes a unit, the standard quantity being counted: centimetres for length, square centimetres for area, and cubic centimetres for volume. The number \(12\) alone does not tell us what was measured.

A conversion factor is a fraction equal to 1 that changes the unit without changing the size, such as \(\frac{12\text{ in}}{1\text{ ft}}\). The SI (metric) system is built on the metre and uses prefixes such as milli-, centi-, and kilo-; the imperial system uses inches, feet, yards, and miles. Precision matters because a tool reports only to its smallest mark, so a length recorded to the nearest millimetre and one recorded to the nearest inch carry different information.

Any equality between two units can be written as a fraction equal to 1. Since \(1\text{ ft}=12\text{ in}\), both \(\frac{12\text{ in}}{1\text{ ft}}\) and \(\frac{1\text{ ft}}{12\text{ in}}\) equal 1. Multiplying by such a fraction changes the units but not the length. Put the unit you want to remove in the denominator so that it cancels.

Imperial Length Conversions

\(1\text{ ft}=12\text{ in}\)

\(1\text{ yd}=3\text{ ft}=36\text{ in}\)

\(1\text{ mi}=1760\text{ yd}=5280\text{ ft}\)

Example 1: Converting Feet to Inches

A room is \(15\text{ ft}\) long. How many inches is this?

Use the fraction with feet on the bottom so the feet cancel:

\(15\text{ ft}\times\frac{12\text{ in}}{1\text{ ft}}=180\text{ in}\)

Motivation: Flooring and curtain fabric are often sold by the inch.

1.2 Precision in Measurement

Measure objects with rulers and tape measures and compare your results with a partner's. First find the value of the smallest mark on the tool, then read to the nearest half or quarter of that mark.

No measurement is exact. The precision of a tool is the smallest difference it can detect, and we round a result to match that precision. A ruler marked in millimetres gives a length like \(12.3\text{ cm}\), not \(12.345678\text{ cm}\). Extra digits suggest accuracy the tool cannot provide.

  • Read measurements to the nearest fraction of an inch, or to the nearest millimetre
  • Use significant figures that match the tool being used
  • Estimate a measurement before taking it, then check how close you were
  • Compare repeated measurements to see the effect of measurement uncertainty

Unit cancellation in four steps:

  1. Write the starting measurement with its unit, such as \(60\text{ in}\).
  2. Choose an equality that links the old unit to the new one: \(1\text{ ft}=12\text{ in}\).
  3. Make a fraction equal to 1 with the old unit on the bottom: \(\frac{1\text{ ft}}{12\text{ in}}\).
  4. Multiply and cancel the old unit: \(60\text{ in}\times\frac{1\text{ ft}}{12\text{ in}}=5\text{ ft}\).

Precision is not accuracy. Precision describes how finely a tool reads, while accuracy describes how close a result is to the true value. A ruler marked in millimetres is precise, but it is accurate only when used correctly. Repeated readings that agree are precise, yet they may all be wrong in the same way.

Common mistakes:

  • Reporting more digits than the measuring tool can actually provide
  • Putting the old unit on top, so it does not cancel with the starting unit
  • Forgetting to write the final unit after the arithmetic is finished

Real-World Connection: Carpenters, engineers, and scientists all need to measure accurately. A small error can mean a part that does not fit or a design that fails.

1.3 Relating SI and Imperial Units

The SI (metric) system is built around the metre. Prefixes tell how large a unit is compared with the base unit: milli- means one thousandth, centi- means one hundredth, and kilo- means one thousand. So \(1\text{ cm}=0.01\text{ m}\) and \(1\text{ km}=1000\text{ m}\).

The link between the two systems is the exact relationship \(1\text{ in}=2.54\text{ cm}\). Write it as a fraction equal to 1 and let the units cancel, just as in Section 1.1.

Conversion Factor

\(1\text{ in}=2.54\text{ cm}\)

Use \(1=\frac{2.54\text{ cm}}{1\text{ in}}\) or \(1=\frac{1\text{ in}}{2.54\text{ cm}}\), depending on the unit you want.

Similarly, \(1\text{ ft}=0.3048\text{ m}\).

Example 2: Converting Metres to Feet

A room is \(4.5\text{ m}\) wide. What is this in feet?

Put metres in the denominator so they cancel:

\(4.5\text{ m}\times\frac{1\text{ ft}}{0.3048\text{ m}}\approx 14.76\text{ ft}\)

Motivation: This helps when reading plans from another country or buying materials sized in feet.

1.4 Surface Areas of Right Pyramids and Right Cones

A right pyramid has a regular polygon for a base, and its apex sits directly above the centre of that base. A right cone has a circular base with its apex directly above the centre of the circle.

To find surface area, imagine cutting the solid open and laying it flat as a net. The total is the base area plus the area of the lateral faces, which are the sides. Each triangular face of a pyramid has area \(\frac{1}{2}(\text{edge})(s)\), where \(s\) is the slant height. Adding all the faces gives \(\frac{1}{2}Ps\), because the edges add up to the base perimeter \(P\).

A cone's lateral surface unrolls into a circular sector, and its area simplifies to \(\pi r l\). Here \(l\) is the slant height along the side of the cone, not the vertical height \(h\).

Reading the formulas: In these formulas, \(r\) is the radius of a circle or sphere, \(h\) is the perpendicular height from the apex straight down to the base, \(s\) or \(l\) is the slant height measured along a side, and \(B\) is the area of the base. Matching each symbol to the correct length is the key first step.

Net versus solid: The solid is the three-dimensional object; its net is the flat pattern made by cutting it open and laying it flat. Surface area measures the outside of the solid, which is the area of the net. Volume measures the space inside the solid, so it cannot be read from the net.

Warning: Use the slant height \(s\) or \(l\) for lateral area, since the side faces are measured along the slant. Use the vertical height \(h\) for volume, since volume depends on perpendicular height. A slant height substituted into a volume formula gives a result that is too large.

Surface Area Formulas

Right pyramid: \(SA=B+\frac{1}{2}Ps\)

Here \(B\) is the base area, \(P\) is the base perimeter, and \(s\) is the slant height.

Right cone: \(SA=\pi r^2+\pi r l\)

Here \(r\) is the radius and \(l\) is the slant height.

Quick check: A square pyramid has base edge \(6\text{ cm}\) and slant height \(5\text{ cm}\), so \(B=36\text{ cm}^2\) and \(P=24\text{ cm}\).

Its lateral area is \(\frac{1}{2}(24)(5)=60\text{ cm}^2\), so \(SA=36+60=96\text{ cm}^2\).

1.5 Volumes of Right Pyramids and Right Cones

Volume measures the space inside a solid, in cubic units such as \(\text{cm}^3\). If a pyramid or cone and a prism or cylinder have the same base and the same perpendicular height, the pointed solid holds exactly one third as much.

You can see this by filling a cone with water and pouring it into a cylinder of equal base and height: it takes three full cones to fill the cylinder. That is where the factor \(\frac{1}{3}\) comes from. The height \(h\) must be measured straight from the apex to the base, not along the slanted side.

Volume Formulas

Right pyramid: \(V=\frac{1}{3}Bh\), where \(B\) is the base area.

Right cone: \(V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h\)

Example 3: Volume of a Traffic Cone

A traffic cone has radius \(10\text{ cm}\) and height \(35\text{ cm}\). Find its volume.

\(V=\frac{1}{3}\pi (10)^2(35)\)

\(V=\frac{3500\pi}{3}\approx 3665\text{ cm}^3\)

Motivation: Manufacturers use this to estimate how much plastic each cone needs.

Worked Example: Volume of a Square Pyramid

A square pyramid has base edge \(6\text{ cm}\) and vertical height \(10\text{ cm}\). Find its volume.

The base is a square, so \(B=6^{2}=36\text{ cm}^{2}\).

\(V=\frac{1}{3}Bh=\frac{1}{3}(36)(10)=120\text{ cm}^{3}\)

Motivation: This estimates the space inside a pyramid-shaped roof or a container with a square base.

Common Mistakes and Considerations:

  • Volume uses cubic units such as \(\text{cm}^{3}\), not \(\text{cm}\) or \(\text{cm}^{2}\)
  • The radius is half the diameter: if \(d=20\text{ cm}\), then \(r=10\text{ cm}\)
  • Use slant height for lateral area, but vertical height for volume
  • Convert all lengths to the same unit before substituting into a formula

1.6 Surface Area and Volume of a Sphere

A sphere is the set of all points in space that are the same distance \(r\) from a centre point, and that distance is the radius. The diameter is \(d=2r\), so divide a diameter by two before using the formulas.

Why Spheres Are Special: For a given volume, a sphere has the smallest possible surface area. That is why bubbles and droplets are round, and why engineers choose spherical shapes when they want to save material.

Sphere Formulas

For a sphere of radius \(r\):

Surface area: \(SA=4\pi r^2\)

Volume: \(V=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\)

Example 4: Volume and Surface Area of a Basketball

A basketball has radius \(12\text{ cm}\). Find its volume and surface area.

First \(12^3=1728\) and \(12^2=144\).

\(V=\frac{4}{3}\pi (12)^3=2304\pi\approx 7238\text{ cm}^3\)

\(SA=4\pi (12)^2=576\pi\approx 1810\text{ cm}^2\)

Motivation: Manufacturers check these values against official size requirements.

Worked Example: Sphere from Its Diameter

A sphere has diameter \(20\text{ cm}\). Find its surface area and volume.

First find the radius: \(r=\frac{d}{2}=\frac{20}{2}=10\text{ cm}\).

\(SA=4\pi (10)^{2}=400\pi\approx 1257\text{ cm}^{2}\)

\(V=\frac{4}{3}\pi (10)^{3}=\frac{4000\pi}{3}\approx 4189\text{ cm}^{3}\)

Check: Area uses square units and volume uses cubic units, and both calculations use the same radius \(r=10\text{ cm}\).

1.7 Solving Problems Involving Objects

Measurement problems are easier when you follow the same routine each time. Sketch the object, write down what is given, and decide whether you need length, area, or volume before choosing a formula.

  1. Identify the shape and the quantity you need to find
  2. Choose the matching formula and write down what each symbol means
  3. Convert every measurement to the same unit, using fractions equal to 1
  4. Substitute the values and calculate, keeping track of the units
  5. Round to a sensible precision and check that the answer is reasonable

For the last step, compare with something familiar: a basketball should have a volume of a few thousand \(\text{cm}^3\), not a few hundred. State the precision of your measuring tool beside your final answer.

Real-World Applications: These skills are used in construction, manufacturing, cooking, and many other fields where precise measurements matter.

1.8 Check Your Understanding

Work on a separate page first, then compare your answers below.

  1. Convert \(60\text{ in}\) to feet using a fraction equal to 1.
  2. A square pyramid has base edge \(6\text{ cm}\) and vertical height \(10\text{ cm}\). Find its volume.
  3. A sphere has diameter \(20\text{ cm}\). State its radius, then find its surface area and volume to the nearest whole unit.
Answers with Brief Work

1. \(60\text{ in}\times\frac{1\text{ ft}}{12\text{ in}}=5\text{ ft}\)

2. \(B=6^{2}=36\text{ cm}^{2}\), so \(V=\frac{1}{3}(36)(10)=120\text{ cm}^{3}\)

3. \(r=10\text{ cm}\), \(SA=400\pi\approx 1257\text{ cm}^{2}\), and \(V=\frac{4000\pi}{3}\approx 4189\text{ cm}^{3}\)

Bonus: How Scaling Affects Area and Volume

If you double every length of a solid, its surface area grows by \(2^2=4\) but its volume grows by \(2^3=8\). That is why the formulas contain \(r^2\) and \(r^3\).

The same idea explains unit conversions: \(1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}\), so \(1\text{ m}^2=10\,000\text{ cm}^2\) and \(1\text{ m}^3=1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3\).
