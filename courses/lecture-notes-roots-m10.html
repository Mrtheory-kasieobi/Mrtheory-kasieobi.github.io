Lecture 4: Roots and Powers

Mathematics 10 - Chapter 4

4.1 The Real Number System

Every number you use in this course belongs to the real number system. We classify numbers by how they can be written: rational numbers can be written as a fraction of integers, while irrational numbers cannot.

The sets are nested: natural numbers sit inside whole numbers, which sit inside integers, which sit inside rational numbers. Rational and irrational numbers together make up the real numbers.

Why This Classification Matters: Knowing the type of number tells us what kind of answer to expect. \(\sqrt{9} = 3\) is rational, but \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational, so its decimal never ends or repeats and we usually leave it as \(\sqrt{2}\).

Number Classification

Natural Numbers: \(1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots\) — the counting numbers.

Whole Numbers: \(0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) — natural numbers plus zero.

Integers: \(\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\) — whole numbers and their negatives.

Rational Numbers: Can be written as \(\frac{a}{b}\) with integers \(a\) and \(b\), where \(b \neq 0\), such as \(\frac{3}{4}\), \(-2\), and \(0.5\).

Irrational Numbers: Cannot be written as fractions; their decimals never end or repeat, such as \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(\pi\).

Real Numbers: Every rational and irrational number together.

Example: Classifying Numbers

Classify \(\sqrt{16}\), \(\frac{-6}{3}\), \(\sqrt{5}\), and \(0.75\).

\(\sqrt{16} = 4\): natural number, whole number, integer, and rational number.

\(\frac{-6}{3} = -2\): integer and rational number.

\(\sqrt{5}\) is irrational because 5 is not a perfect square.

\(0.75 = \frac{3}{4}\) is rational because the decimal terminates.

Classifying more examples:

  • \(0.333\ldots=\frac{1}{3}\) is rational because the decimal repeats
  • \(\sqrt{7}\) is irrational because 7 is not a perfect square
  • \(-5\) is an integer and a rational number
  • \(0\) is a whole number, an integer, and a rational number
  • \(\pi\) is irrational because its decimal never ends or repeats

4.2 Simplifying Powers

A power such as \(2^{4}\) means repeated multiplication: \(2^{4} = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 16\). The base is 2, and the exponent 4 tells us how many factors of 2 to multiply.

Connection to Real World: Powers describe repeated growth, such as doubling an amount of money over several time periods, or counting possible outcomes when several independent choices are made in a row.

A perfect square is a number such as \(49\) that equals an integer multiplied by itself: \(7^{2}=49\). A perfect cube equals an integer multiplied by itself three times: \((-3)^{3}=-27\). Roots undo powers, so recognising these numbers makes roots quick to evaluate.

Perfect Squares, Cubes, and Principal Roots

Perfect squares: \(1,4,9,16,25,36,49,64,81,100\)

Perfect cubes: \(1,8,27,64,125\) and \(-1,-8,-27,-64,-125\)

Principal square root: \(\sqrt{a}\) is the non-negative number whose square is \(a\), so \(\sqrt{49}=7\), not \(\pm 7\).

Cube root: \(\sqrt[3]{-27}=-3\) because \((-3)^{3}=-27\). Unlike square roots, cube roots may be negative.

Laws of Exponents from Repeated Multiplication

Product Rule: \(a^{3} \times a^{2} = (a \times a \times a)(a \times a) = a^{5}\), so \(a^{m} \times a^{n} = a^{m+n}\).

Quotient Rule: \(\frac{a^{5}}{a^{2}} = \frac{a \times a \times a \times a \times a}{a \times a} = a^{3}\), so \(\frac{a^{m}}{a^{n}} = a^{m-n}\).

Power Rule: \((a^{2})^{3} = (a \times a)(a \times a)(a \times a) = a^{6}\), so \((a^{m})^{n} = a^{mn}\).

These rules work because we are counting factors of the same base, so we never combine different bases this way.

Example 1: Simplifying Powers

Simplify: \(\frac{(x^{3} y^{-2})^{4}}{x^{5} y^{2}}\).

Apply the power rule first: \((x^{3})^{4} = x^{12}\) and \((y^{-2})^{4} = y^{-8}\).

Subtract exponents of like bases: \(x^{12} \div x^{5} = x^{7}\) and \(y^{-8} \div y^{2} = y^{-10}\).

Write with positive exponents: \(x^{7} y^{-10} = \frac{x^{7}}{y^{10}}\).

Check: Substitute \(x = 2\) and \(y = 1\). The original gives \(8^{4} \div 32 = 128\), and the answer gives \(2^{7} = 128\).

4.3 Exponent Laws with Numeric Bases

Patterns with numbers make the exponent laws easy to see. Each time the exponent drops by 1, we divide by the base. Here are the powers of 2:

\[2^{4} = 16,\quad 2^{3} = 8,\quad 2^{2} = 4,\quad 2^{1} = 2.\]

Continuing the pattern gives \(2^{0} = 2 \div 2 = 1\) and \(2^{-1} = 1 \div 2 = \frac{1}{2}\). In general, \(a^{0} = 1\) and \(a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^{n}}\) for \(a \neq 0\).

Check With Numbers: \(3^{2} \times 3^{3} = 9 \times 27 = 243\) and \(3^{5} = 243\). Also, \(2^{5} \div 2^{2} = 32 \div 4 = 8 = 2^{3}\).

Everyday Connection: Each step in a base-ten place-value chart multiplies by \(10\): \(0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1\).

Step-by-step patterns:

  1. To multiply powers of the same base, write the factors and count them: \(2^{3}\times 2^{2}=(2\times 2\times 2)(2\times 2)=2^{5}\). Exponents add.
  2. To raise a power to a power, count the groups of factors: \((2^{3})^{2}=2^{3}\times 2^{3}=2^{6}\). Exponents multiply.
  3. To divide, cancel matching top and bottom factors: \(2^{5}\div 2^{2}=2^{3}\). Exponents subtract.

Same base required: these rules work only when the bases are identical. \(2^{3}\times 3^{2}\) cannot be combined by adding exponents: \(8\times 9=72\), which is not \(6^{5}\). Rewrite bases when you can: \(4\times 2^{3}=2^{2}\times 2^{3}=32\).

4.4 Checking Exponent Laws with Patterns

Test the exponent laws with numerical examples. Try several bases and exponents, and check that both sides of each rule give the same value.

Key Insight: The exponent laws work because a power counts repeated factors. Multiplying \(a^{m} \times a^{n}\) combines \(m\) factors and \(n\) factors for \(m + n\) factors in total, while dividing cancels common factors.

Four concrete checks:

  • Product rule: \(2^{3}\cdot 2^{2}=8\times 4=32=2^{5}\)
  • Power rule: \((3^{2})^{3}=9^{3}=729=3^{6}\)
  • Zero exponent: \(5^{0}=1\)
  • Negative exponent: \(4^{-1}=\frac{1}{4}\)

Each check matches the rule exactly. Trying a few more examples with a calculator is a good way to catch mistakes before using the laws with variables.

4.5 Negative Exponents and Reciprocals

A negative exponent tells us to take a reciprocal; it does not make the answer negative. For example, \(2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^{3}} = \frac{1}{8}\), while \(-2^{3} = -8\).

Why This Makes Sense: The product rule should still work: \(a^{3} \times a^{-3} = a^{3 + (-3)} = a^{0} = 1\). This forces \(a^{-3} = \frac{1}{a^{3}}\), because a number times its reciprocal is 1.

Negative exponents also describe small numbers in scientific notation: since \(10^{-4}=\frac{1}{10^{4}}\), we can write \(0.00042=4.2\times 10^{-4}\). We return to this notation in Section 4.8.

Example 2: Working with Negative Exponents

Simplify: \(\frac{3x^{-2}y^{3}}{2x^{4}y^{-1}}\).

Move factors with negative exponents across the fraction bar: \(x^{-2} = \frac{1}{x^{2}}\) and \(y^{-1} = \frac{1}{y}\).

Group like bases: \(\frac{3y^{3} \times y}{2x^{4} \times x^{2}}\).

Combine the factors: \(\frac{3y^{4}}{2x^{6}}\).

Check: With \(x = 1\) and \(y = 2\), the original is \(24 \div 1 = 24\); the answer is \(\frac{3 \times 16}{2} = 24\).

4.6 Evaluating Powers with Rational Bases

To raise a fraction to a power, raise the numerator and the denominator to that power separately: \(\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{n} = \frac{a^{n}}{b^{n}}\). This is just repeated multiplication of the fraction.

Example: Powers of Fractions

Evaluate \(\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{3}\).

\(\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{3} = \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{8}{27}\).

Check: \(2^{3} = 8\) and \(3^{3} = 27\), so the result is \(\frac{8}{27}\).

Application: If the chance of heads on one toss is \(\frac{1}{2}\), then the chance of three heads in a row is \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{3} = \frac{1}{8}\).

Example: Applying Root and Exponent Rules Together

Simplify \(\frac{(\sqrt{16})^{3}\times 2^{-2}}{2^{1}}\).

Step 1: Evaluate the root: \(\sqrt{16}=4\), and \(4^{3}=64=2^{6}\).

Step 2: Apply the product rule: \(2^{6}\times 2^{-2}=2^{6+(-2)}=2^{4}=16\).

Step 3: Apply the quotient rule: \(2^{4}\div 2^{1}=2^{4-1}=2^{3}=8\).

Check: Directly, \((64\times \frac{1}{4})\div 2=16\div 2=8\).

4.7 Combining Laws of Exponents

Complicated expressions may need several exponent laws. Simplify the innermost brackets first, combine like bases by adding or subtracting exponents, and finish by writing answers with positive exponents.

Example 3: Complex Power Simplification

Simplify: \(\left(\frac{a^{-2}b^{3}}{a^{4}b^{-1}}\right)^{-2}\).

Step 1: Simplify inside the brackets: \(a^{-2-4} = a^{-6}\) and \(b^{3-(-1)} = b^{4}\), giving \(a^{-6}b^{4}\).

Step 2: Apply the outer exponent: \((a^{-6})^{-2} = a^{12}\) and \((b^{4})^{-2} = b^{-8}\).

Step 3: Write with a positive exponent: \(a^{12}b^{-8} = \frac{a^{12}}{b^{8}}\).

Check: With \(a = 1\) and \(b = 2\), the original is \(16^{-2} = \frac{1}{256}\), and the answer is \(\frac{1}{2^{8}} = \frac{1}{256}\).

Motivation: These steps are useful whenever a formula contains several powers of the same quantities, such as geometry formulas for area and volume.

Common Mistakes and Considerations:

  • \(a^{m}\times a^{n}=a^{m+n}\), not \(a^{mn}\): add exponents when multiplying
  • \((a^{m})^{n}=a^{mn}\), not \(a^{m+n}\): multiply exponents for a power of a power
  • \(a^{0}=1\) for \(a\neq 0\); it is not 0 and \(0^{0}\) is not used here
  • \(a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^{n}}\); a negative exponent does not make the answer negative
  • Rules such as \(a^{m}\times a^{n}=a^{m+n}\) need the same base, so \(2^{3}\times 3^{2}\) cannot be combined this way
  • \(\sqrt{49}=7\) is the principal root only; \(\sqrt[3]{-27}=-3\) is allowed because cubes can be negative

4.8 Scientific Notation

Scientific notation writes a number as \(a \times 10^{n}\), where \(1 \leq a < 10\) and \(n\) is an integer. It keeps very large and very small numbers short and makes their size easy to compare.

Examples: \(1\,500\,000 = 1.5 \times 10^{6}\) and \(0.0004 = 4 \times 10^{-4}\). The distance to the Sun is about \(1.5 \times 10^{8}\) km.

Scientific Notation Operations

To multiply: \((a \times 10^{m}) \times (b \times 10^{n}) = (ab) \times 10^{m+n}\).

To divide: \(\frac{a \times 10^{m}}{b \times 10^{n}} = \frac{a}{b} \times 10^{m-n}\).

If the front number becomes 10 or more, move the decimal and add 1 to the exponent.

Example: Multiplying and Dividing in Scientific Notation

Multiply \((3 \times 10^{5}) \times (2 \times 10^{3})\).

Multiply the front numbers and add the exponents: \(3 \times 2 = 6\) and \(10^{5} \times 10^{3} = 10^{8}\). The product is \(6 \times 10^{8}\).

Now divide \((6 \times 10^{8}) \div (2 \times 10^{3})\).

Divide the front numbers and subtract the exponents: \(6 \div 2 = 3\) and \(10^{8} \div 10^{3} = 10^{5}\). The quotient is \(3 \times 10^{5}\).

Check in ordinary form: \(600\,000\,000 \div 2\,000 = 300\,000 = 3 \times 10^{5}\).

Worked conversions:

  1. Move the decimal point in \(0.00042\) four places to the right to get \(4.2\), so \(0.00042=4.2\times 10^{-4}\).
  2. Move the decimal point in \(7\,800\,000\) six places to the left to get \(7.8\), so \(7\,800\,000=7.8\times 10^{6}\).

Quick division: \(7.8\times 10^{6}\) divided by \(4.2\times 10^{-4}\) gives \(\frac{7.8}{4.2}\times 10^{6-(-4)}\approx 1.86\times 10^{10}\).

4.9 Check Your Understanding

Work on a separate page first, then compare your answers below.

  1. Evaluate \(\sqrt{49}\) and \(\sqrt[3]{-27}\).
  2. Simplify \(2^{3}\cdot 2^{2}\), then write \(0.00042\) in scientific notation.
  3. Simplify \(\frac{x^{5}y^{-2}}{x^{2}y^{3}}\) using positive exponents only.
Answers with Brief Work

1. \(\sqrt{49}=7\) (the principal, non-negative root) and \(\sqrt[3]{-27}=-3\).

2. \(2^{3}\cdot 2^{2}=2^{5}=32\), and \(0.00042=4.2\times 10^{-4}\).

3. Subtract exponents of like bases: \(x^{5-2}=x^{3}\) and \(y^{-2-3}=y^{-5}\), so the result is \(\frac{x^{3}}{y^{5}}\).

Bonus: Radicals as Powers

A root can be written as a fractional exponent: \(\sqrt{a} = a^{\frac{1}{2}}\) and, more generally, \(\sqrt[n]{a} = a^{\frac{1}{n}}\). This makes sense because \(\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{a} = a\), just as \(a^{\frac{1}{2}} \times a^{\frac{1}{2}} = a^{1} = a\) by the product rule.

For example, \(\sqrt[3]{8} = 8^{\frac{1}{3}} = 2\) because \(2^{3} = 8\). The exponent rules then work on roots too: \((\sqrt{9})^{2} = (9^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2} = 9\).
