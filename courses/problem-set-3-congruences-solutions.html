Solutions: Problem Set 3: Congruences

Elementary Number Theory Medium

Solutions

Problem 1

Solution: We need to solve \(3x \equiv 7 \pmod{11}\). Since \(\gcd(3, 11) = 1\), 3 has a multiplicative inverse modulo 11.

Step 1: Find \(3^{-1} \pmod{11}\). Try values: \(3 \cdot 4 = 12 \equiv 1 \pmod{11}\), so \(3^{-1} \equiv 4 \pmod{11}\).

Step 2: Multiply both sides by 4: \(x \equiv 4 \cdot 7 \equiv 28 \equiv 6 \pmod{11}\).

Conclusion: \(x \equiv 6 \pmod{11}\).

Problem 2

Solution: We need to find all \(x\) such that \(x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{8}\).

Step 1: Test all residues modulo 8: \(0^2 = 0\), \(1^2 = 1\), \(2^2 = 4\), \(3^2 = 9 \equiv 1\), \(4^2 = 16 \equiv 0\), \(5^2 = 25 \equiv 1\), \(6^2 = 36 \equiv 4\), \(7^2 = 49 \equiv 1\).

Conclusion: The solutions are \(x \equiv 1, 3, 5, 7 \pmod{8}\), i.e., all odd residues.

Problem 3

Solution: If \(a \equiv b \pmod{m}\) and \(c \equiv d \pmod{m}\), then \(a - b = mk\) and \(c - d = ml\) for some integers \(k, l\).

Step 1: Consider \(a + c - (b + d) = (a - b) + (c - d) = mk + ml = m(k + l)\).

Step 2: This shows \(a + c \equiv b + d \pmod{m}\).

Conclusion: Congruences are preserved under addition.

Problem 4

Solution: Find \(2^{100} \pmod{11}\).

Step 1: By Fermat's Little Theorem, since 11 is prime and \(\gcd(2, 11) = 1\), we have \(2^{10} \equiv 1 \pmod{11}\).

Step 2: Write \(100 = 10 \cdot 10\), so \(2^{100} = (2^{10})^{10} \equiv 1^{10} = 1 \pmod{11}\).

Conclusion: \(2^{100} \equiv 1 \pmod{11}\).

Problem 5

Solution: Determine if 5 has a multiplicative inverse modulo 14.

Step 1: Check \(\gcd(5, 14)\). Since \(14 = 5 \cdot 2 + 4\), \(5 = 4 \cdot 1 + 1\), we have \(\gcd(5, 14) = 1\).

Step 2: Since the gcd is 1, an inverse exists. Find it by testing: \(5 \cdot 1 = 5\), \(5 \cdot 2 = 10\), \(5 \cdot 3 = 15 \equiv 1 \pmod{14}\).

Conclusion: The inverse of 5 modulo 14 is 3.

Problem 6

Solution: Solve the system: \(x \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\) and \(x \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\).

Step 1: From the first congruence, \(x = 2 + 3k\) for some integer \(k\).

Step 2: Substitute into the second: \(2 + 3k \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\), so \(3k \equiv 1 \pmod{5}\).

Step 3: Find \(3^{-1} \pmod{5}\): \(3 \cdot 2 = 6 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}\), so \(k \equiv 2 \pmod{5}\).

Step 4: Thus \(k = 2 + 5t\) and \(x = 2 + 3(2 + 5t) = 8 + 15t\).

Conclusion: \(x \equiv 8 \pmod{15}\).

Problem 7

Solution: Prove Fermat's Little Theorem: \(a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\) for prime \(p\) and \(p \nmid a\).

Step 1: Consider the set \(S = \{a, 2a, 3a, \ldots, (p-1)a\}\).

Step 2: None of these elements are congruent to 0 modulo \(p\) since \(p \nmid a\).

Step 3: If \(ia \equiv ja \pmod{p}\) for \(1 \leq i < j \leq p-1\), then \(p \mid a(j-i)\), so \(p \mid (j-i)\), contradicting \(1 \leq j-i \leq p-2\).

Step 4: Thus \(S\) contains \(p-1\) distinct non-zero residues modulo \(p\).

Step 5: Since \(\{1, 2, \ldots, p-1\}\) also has \(p-1\) distinct non-zero residues, \(S \equiv \{1, 2, \ldots, p-1\} \pmod{p}\).

Step 6: Therefore, \(a \cdot 2a \cdot 3a \cdots (p-1)a \equiv 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots (p-1) \pmod{p}\).

Step 7: This gives \(a^{p-1} \cdot (p-1)! \equiv (p-1)! \pmod{p}\).

Step 8: Cancel \((p-1)!\) (which is coprime to \(p\)): \(a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\).

Conclusion: Fermat's Little Theorem is proved.

Problem 8

Solution: Find integers \(x\) such that \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{2}\), \(x \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\), and \(x \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\).

Step 1: From \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{2}\), write \(x = 1 + 2k\).

Step 2: Substitute: \(1 + 2k \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\), so \(2k \equiv 1 \pmod{3}\). Since \(2^{-1} \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\), we get \(k \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\).

Step 3: Write \(k = 2 + 3m\), so \(x = 1 + 2(2 + 3m) = 5 + 6m\).

Step 4: Substitute into the third congruence: \(5 + 6m \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\), so \(6m \equiv -2 \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\), giving \(m \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\).

Step 5: Write \(m = 3 + 5t\), so \(x = 5 + 6(3 + 5t) = 23 + 30t\).

Conclusion: \(x \equiv 23 \pmod{30}\).

Problem 9

Solution: Prove Wilson's theorem: \((p-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod{p}\) for prime \(p\).

Step 1: Consider the multiplicative group \(\mathbb{Z}_p^* = \{1, 2, \ldots, p-1\}\) under multiplication modulo \(p\).

Step 2: Each element has a unique multiplicative inverse in this group.

Step 3: The only elements that are their own inverses are 1 and \(p-1 \equiv -1 \pmod{p}\), since \(x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\) implies \(x \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{p}\).

Step 4: Pair up the remaining elements: \(2 \cdot 2^{-1} \equiv 1\), \(3 \cdot 3^{-1} \equiv 1\), etc.

Step 5: The product of all elements is: \(1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots (p-1) \equiv 1 \cdot (p-1) \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdots 1 \equiv p-1 \equiv -1 \pmod{p}\).

Conclusion: Wilson's theorem is proved.

Problem 10

Solution: Show that \(n^3 - n\) is divisible by 6 for all integers \(n\).

Step 1: Factor: \(n^3 - n = n(n^2 - 1) = n(n-1)(n+1)\), the product of three consecutive integers.

Step 2: Among any three consecutive integers, one is divisible by 3 (since residues modulo 3 are 0, 1, 2).

Step 3: Among any two consecutive integers, one is divisible by 2. Among three consecutive integers, at least one is even.

Step 4: Therefore, the product \(n(n-1)(n+1)\) is divisible by both 2 and 3.

Step 5: Since \(\gcd(2, 3) = 1\), the product is divisible by \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\).

Conclusion: \(6 \mid (n^3 - n)\) for all integers \(n\).

Bonus Problems

Bonus Problem 1

Solution:

Step 1: We want to find the last two digits of \(7^{100}\), i.e., compute \(7^{100} \pmod{100}\).

Step 2: Note that \(100 = 4 \cdot 25\). We'll use the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

Step 3: Compute \(7^{100} \pmod{4}\): \(7 \equiv 3 \pmod{4}\), and \(3^2 = 9 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\), so \(7^{100} = (7^2)^{50} \equiv 1^{50} = 1 \pmod{4}\).

Step 4: Compute \(7^{100} \pmod{25}\): By Euler's theorem, \(\phi(25) = 20\), so \(7^{20} \equiv 1 \pmod{25}\). Thus \(7^{100} = (7^{20})^5 \equiv 1^5 = 1 \pmod{25}\).

Step 5: We need \(x\) such that \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\) and \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{25}\).

Step 6: Since \(x \equiv 1\) modulo both 4 and 25, and \(\gcd(4, 25) = 1\), by CRT we have \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{100}\).

Conclusion: The last two digits of \(7^{100}\) are 01.

Bonus Problem 2

Solution:

Step 1: We prove by induction on \(n\) that if \(a \equiv b \pmod{m}\), then \(a^n \equiv b^n \pmod{m}\) for all \(n \geq 1\).

Step 2: Base case \(n = 1\): \(a^1 = a \equiv b = b^1 \pmod{m}\).

Step 3: Inductive hypothesis: Assume \(a^k \equiv b^k \pmod{m}\) for some \(k \geq 1\).

Step 4: Inductive step: \(a^{k+1} = a^k \cdot a \equiv b^k \cdot b = b^{k+1} \pmod{m}\), where we used the fact that if \(x \equiv x'\) and \(y \equiv y'\), then \(xy \equiv x'y' \pmod{m}\).

Conclusion: By mathematical induction, \(a^n \equiv b^n \pmod{m}\) for all \(n \geq 1\).

Bonus Problem 3

Solution:

Step 1: Suppose \(x^2 \equiv a \pmod{p}\) has solutions. We show there are at most 2.

Step 2: If \(r\) is a solution, then \(x^2 - r^2 \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\), so \((x-r)(x+r) \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\).

Step 3: Since \(p\) is prime, \(p \mid (x-r)(x+r)\) implies \(p \mid (x-r)\) or \(p \mid (x+r)\).

Step 4: Thus \(x \equiv r \pmod{p}\) or \(x \equiv -r \pmod{p}\).

Step 5: These are at most two distinct solutions (they coincide when \(r \equiv -r \pmod{p}\), i.e., \(2r \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\), which means \(r \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\) since \(p \neq 2\)).

Conclusion: The congruence \(x^2 \equiv a \pmod{p}\) has at most 2 solutions.
