Worked Solutions

Each solution gives one complete route and notes where a common error can occur.

  1. 1. The statement is indexed by \(n\ge1\), so the first case to verify is \(n=1\): \(1=1(2)/2\).
  2. 2. To prove \(P(n)\to P(n+1)\), we assume \(P(n)\) for an arbitrary \(n\ge1\) and derive \(P(n+1)\). We do not assume the conclusion \(P(n+1)\).
  3. 3. Strong induction assumes \(P(k)\) for all previous values \(k<n\), up to whatever lower bound the statement requires. This is more informative than ordinary induction, which assumes only \(P(n)\).
  4. 4. The well-ordering principle for \(\mathbb{N}\) says that every nonempty subset has a least element. It need not have a maximum and need not contain 0.
  5. 5. \(F_3=F_2+F_1=2\), \(F_4=F_3+F_2=3\), and \(F_5=F_4+F_3=5\).
  6. 6. Euclid's lemma is the key prime property: if a prime divides a product, it divides at least one factor. The other options misstate the result.
  7. 7. \(17=3\cdot5+2\), with \(0\le2<5\), so \(17\bmod5=2\).
  8. 8. \(\binom52=\frac{5!}{2!3!}=10\). Equivalently, there are 10 two-element subsets of a five-element set.
  9. 9. An injection \(A\to\mathbb{N}\) embeds \(A\) into the natural numbers, so \(A\) is finite or countably infinite. A subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) need not be countable, such as \((0,1)\).
  10. 10. Cantor's theorem says no set has the same cardinality as its power set; the injection \(a\mapsto\{a\}\) gives \(|A|\le|\mathcal{P}(A)|\), and the diagonal argument rules out a bijection.
  11. 11. Base case: \(1=\frac{1\cdot2}{2}\). Inductive step: assume \(\sum_{i=1}^n i=\frac{n(n+1)}2\). Then \(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}i=\frac{n(n+1)}2+(n+1)=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2\), which is the formula for \(n+1\).
  12. 12. \(n^3-n=n(n-1)(n+1)\), the product of three consecutive integers. Among any two consecutive integers one is even, and among any three consecutive integers one is divisible by 3. Since \(\gcd(2,3)=1\), the product is divisible by \(6\).
  13. 13. Base case: \(2\) is prime. Inductive step: assume every integer from \(2\) up to \(n\) is a product of primes. If \(n+1\) is prime, we are done. If \(n+1\) is composite, write \(n+1=ab\) with \(2\le a,b\le n\). By the induction hypothesis, \(a\) and \(b\) are products of primes, so their product \(n+1\) is also a product of primes.
  14. 14. Suppose there is an \(n\ge2\) with no prime divisor. Let \(m\) be the least such integer by well-ordering. Then \(m\) is not prime, so \(m=ab\) with \(1<a<m\) and \(1<b<m\). By minimality, \(a\) has a prime divisor \(p\). Since \(p\mid a\) and \(a\mid m\), we get \(p\mid m\), a contradiction.
  15. 15. Since \(a^2=a\cdot a\), Euclid's lemma applied to the prime \(p\) gives \(p\mid a\) or \(p\mid a\). In either case, \(p\mid a\).
  16. 16. The hypotheses give \(n\mid(a-b)\) and \(n\mid(c-d)\). Since \((a+c)-(b+d)=(a-b)+(c-d)\), \(n\) divides \((a+c)-(b+d)\), which means \(a+c\equiv b+d\pmod n\).
  17. 17. Count all subsets of \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\) in two ways. Grouping subsets by their size \(k\) gives \(\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk\). Deciding independently for each element whether to include it gives \(2^n\) subsets. Hence the two quantities are equal.
  18. 18. Every rational can be written as \(p/q\) with \(p\in\mathbb{Z}\) and \(q\in\mathbb{N}\). The set \(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}\) is countable, so list these pairs and delete repetitions such as \(1/2=2/4\). The remaining list contains every rational exactly once, so \(\mathbb{Q}\) is countable.
  19. 19. Suppose \((0,1)\) were countable and list it as \(x_1,x_2,\ldots\). Write \(x_i=0.d_{i1}d_{i2}\ldots\), choosing the expansion with no trailing nines. Define \(y=0.e_1e_2\ldots\) by \(e_i=1\) if \(d_{ii}\ne1\) and \(e_i=2\) if \(d_{ii}=1\). Then \(y\in(0,1)\) and \(y\) differs from \(x_i\) in the \(i\)-th decimal place, so \(y\) is missing from the list, a contradiction.
  20. 20. The phrase “let \(n=2k\)” assumes the desired conclusion that \(n\) is even; the given information \(n^2\) even does not by itself license that step. Correct strategies: use Euclid's lemma with \(p=2\) applied to \(n\cdot n\), or prove the contrapositive by showing that \(n\) odd implies \(n^2\) odd.
  21. 21. Split a \(2n\)-element set into two halves \(X\) and \(Y\), each of size \(n\). The right side counts all \(n\)-element subsets of \(X\cup Y\). The left side counts the same subsets by choosing \(k\) elements from \(X\) and \(n-k\) elements from \(Y\), giving \(\binom nk\binom n{n-k}=\binom nk^2\) choices for each \(k\). Summing over \(k\) gives the identity.
