Lecture 6: Proof by Contradiction

Introduction to Proofs · Week 6

Lecture 6Proof by Contradiction

Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

  • Explain the logical basis of proof by contradiction.
  • Negate quantified and conditional hypotheses accurately before assuming them.
  • Write contradiction proofs of irrationality and nonexistence.
  • Use contradiction to prove uniqueness statements.
  • Compare contradiction with contrapositive and choose the more natural method.
Beginner's bridge

A contradiction proof assumes that the desired statement is false and derives an impossible consequence. The goal is not to find a numerical contradiction by accident; it is to show that the opposite assumption cannot coexist with known facts.

The classic example is \(\sqrt2\). Assuming \(\sqrt2=a/b\) in lowest terms forces both \(a\) and \(b\) to be even, contradicting lowest terms. The contradiction is not “even numbers are bad”; it is that a reduced fraction cannot have both numerator and denominator divisible by \(2\).

1. The logic of contradiction

To prove a statement \(q\), assume \(\neg q\) and derive a contradiction, usually a statement of the form \(r\land\neg r\). Since \(\neg q\) leads to an impossibility, \(\neg q\) must be false. Under the classical law of excluded middle, every proposition is either true or false, so \(q\) must be true.

In symbols, the rule is: from \((\neg q\to F)\) infer \(q\), where \(F\) denotes a false statement. Its validity follows from the tautology \((\neg q\to F)\to q\), whose truth table is left as an exercise. For an implication \(p\to q\), a contradiction proof assumes both \(p\) and \(\neg q\), and derives a contradiction; this is valid because it proves that \(p\land\neg q\) is impossible, i.e. \(p\to q\).

Standard template. (i) Suppose, for contradiction, that \(\neg q\) holds. (ii) Use definitions and known theorems to derive consequences. (iii) Obtain a statement that contradicts either \(\neg q\), an assumption, or a known fact. (iv) Conclude that \(q\) holds.
Careful with negation. The negation of \(\forall x\,P(x)\) is \(\exists x\,\neg P(x)\), not \(\forall x\,\neg P(x)\). The negation of \(p\to q\) is \(p\land\neg q\), not \(p\to\neg q\). Review Lectures 1 and 2 before writing a contradiction proof involving quantifiers.

2. A classic example: irrationality of \(\sqrt2\)

Definition 6.1 A real number is rational if it equals \(a/b\) for integers \(a,b\) with \(b\ne0\), and can be written so that \(\gcd(a,b)=1\). It is irrational otherwise.
Theorem 6.2 \(\sqrt2\) is irrational.
Proof. Suppose, for contradiction, that \(\sqrt2=a/b\) where \(a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\), \(b\ne0\), and \(\gcd(a,b)=1\). Squaring gives \(a^2=2b^2\), so \(a^2\) is even, and by Theorem 5.5, \(a\) is even. Write \(a=2k\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\). Then \[4k^2=2b^2,\qquad\text{so}\qquad b^2=2k^2.\] Thus \(b^2\) is even, so \(b\) is even. But then \(2\mid a\) and \(2\mid b\), contradicting \(\gcd(a,b)=1\). Therefore \(\sqrt2\) is irrational. \(\square\)

The contradiction is not merely "\(a\) and \(b\) are both even"; it is the violation of the specific reduction assumption \(\gcd(a,b)=1\). Without that assumption, the argument would only show that any representation has both numerator and denominator even, and one could keep dividing by \(2\) forever—an infinite descent, which is another valid way to finish the proof.

3. Infinitude of primes and nonexistence statements

Theorem 6.3 There are infinitely many prime numbers.
Proof. Suppose, for contradiction, that there are only finitely many primes \(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n\). Let \[N=p_1p_2\cdots p_n+1.\\] By the fundamental theorem of arithmetic (proved in Lecture 8), \(N\) has a prime divisor \(p\). But \(p\) must be one of the listed primes \(p_i\), since the list is assumed complete. Then \(p\mid p_1p_2\cdots p_n\) and \(p\mid N\), so \(p\mid(N-p_1p_2\cdots p_n)=1\), which is impossible for a prime. This contradiction shows there are infinitely many primes. \(\square\)

Note that \(N\) itself need not be prime: \(2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13+1=30031=59\cdot509\). We only need the existence of a prime divisor, not that \(N\) is prime. This is a frequent source of incorrect presentations.

Proof by contradiction is also natural for statements of the form "no object with property \(P\) exists": assume such an object exists and derive an impossibility.

4. Uniqueness proofs

To prove "there is at most one object with property \(P\)", assume that \(x\) and \(y\) both have property \(P\), and prove \(x=y\). This is often presented as a contradiction proof: assume \(x\ne y\) and derive a contradiction.

Theorem 6.4 The real equation \(x^3+x=0\) has exactly one real solution.
Proof. Existence: \(x=0\) satisfies the equation. Uniqueness: suppose \(a\) and \(b\) are real solutions with \(a\ne b\). Then \(a^3+a=0=b^3+b\), so \[0=(a^3-b^3)+(a-b)=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2+1).\] Since \(a\ne b\), we may divide by \(a-b\) to get \(a^2+ab+b^2+1=0\). But \[a^2+ab+b^2+1=\left(a+\tfrac b2\right)^2+\tfrac34b^2+1\ge1>0,\] a contradiction. Hence \(a=b\), so the solution is unique. \(\square\)

An alternative direct proof notes that \(x^3+x=x(x^2+1)\) and \(x^2+1>0\) for all real \(x\), so \(x=0\) is the only real root. Choosing between methods is a matter of clarity: contradiction was not necessary here, and the direct factorisation is shorter.

5. Contradiction versus contrapositive

Both methods prove an implication by assuming its negation. For \(p\to q\):

  • Contrapositive: assume \(\neg q\) and derive \(\neg p\). The proof ends with the exact negation of the original hypothesis.
  • Contradiction: assume \(p\land\neg q\) and derive any impossibility. The proof may use the hypothesis and the negation of the conclusion together.

Every contrapositive proof can be rewritten as a contradiction proof: once \(\neg p\) is derived, it contradicts the assumed \(p\). The reverse is not always as clean, because a contradiction may arise from a fact unrelated to \(p\). In Theorem 6.2 the contradiction is with coprimality, not directly with a hypothesis about rationality, so contradiction is the more natural framework.

Guideline If the negation of the conclusion has a useful algebraic form, try contrapositive first. If the negation of the conclusion combines with a hypothesis to produce a contradiction (as with coprimality, minimality, or a positive quantity equal to zero), use contradiction.

6. Extra detail: contradiction versus contrapositive

Contrapositive for \(P\to Q\): assume \(\neg Q\) and derive \(\neg P\).
Contradiction for \(P\to Q\): assume \(P\land\neg Q\) and derive any impossibility.

Every contrapositive proof can be rewritten as a contradiction proof: after deriving \(\neg P\), contradict the assumed \(P\). Contradiction is often shorter for existence, nonexistence, and infinitude claims, where there is no convenient contrapositive form.

Theorem 6.10 There is no largest even integer.
Proof. Suppose \(M\) were the largest even integer. Then \(M+2\) is even and \(M+2>M\), contradicting maximality. Hence no largest even integer exists. \(\square\)
Exercises
  1. Prove by contradiction that there is no largest integer.
  2. Prove that \(\sqrt3\) is irrational.
  3. Prove by contradiction: if \(n^2\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd.
  4. Write the negation of "there exists a unique real \(x\) with \(x^2=0\)" and explain how to disprove it.
  5. Determine whether there is a rational number \(r\) with \(r^2=4/9\) and \(r<0\), and justify your answer.
  6. Give a direct proof and a contradiction proof that if \(3n+1\) is even, then \(n\) is odd.
  7. Prove that the solution to \(2x+5=11\) is unique.
  8. Explain why a proof by contradiction of \(p o q\) may assume \(p\) in addition to \( eg q\).
