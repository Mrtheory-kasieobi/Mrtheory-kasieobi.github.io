Lecture 9: Divisibility and Modular Arithmetic

Introduction to Proofs · Week 9

Lecture 9Divisibility and Modular Arithmetic

Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

  • Use divisibility and congruence definitions fluently in proofs.
  • Prove Euclid's lemma and apply it to uniqueness of prime factorisation.
  • Perform arithmetic in \(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\) and solve simple congruences.
  • Prove statements about squares modulo small integers.
  • State and prove Fermat's little theorem via the binomial theorem.
Beginner's bridge

The notation \(a\mid b\) means “\(a\) divides \(b\)”, i.e. there exists an integer \(k\) with \(b=ak\). Divisibility statements are existence statements, so proofs usually exhibit the integer \(k\).

Congruence \(a\equiv b\pmod n\) means \(n\mid(a-b)\), equivalently \(a\) and \(b\) have the same remainder after division by \(n\). For example, \(17\equiv2\pmod5\) because \(17-2=15\) is divisible by \(5\). Modular arithmetic is ordinary arithmetic performed on remainders.

1. Divisibility and gcd

Definition 9.1 For integers \(a,b\), \(a\mid b\) means \(b=ak\) for some \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\). The greatest common divisor \(\gcd(a,b)\) is the largest positive integer dividing both \(a\) and \(b\); \(\gcd(a,b)=1\) means \(a,b\) are coprime.
Theorem 9.2 (Euclid's lemma) If \(p\) is prime and \(p\mid ab\), then \(p\mid a\) or \(p\mid b\).
Proof. Let \(d=\gcd(p,a)\). Since \(p\) is prime, \(d=1\) or \(d=p\). If \(d=p\), then \(p\mid a\). If \(d=1\), Bézout's identity (Lecture 5, Problem 8) gives integers \(x,y\) with \(px+ay=1\). Multiplying by \(b\), \[pbx+aby=b.\] Since \(p\mid pbx\) and \(p\mid ab\) by hypothesis, \(p\) divides the sum \(pbx+aby=b\). Thus \(p\mid b\). \(\square\)
Theorem 9.3 (Uniqueness in the FTA) If \(n\ge2\) has prime factorisations \[n=p_1p_2\cdots p_r=q_1q_2\cdots q_s\] with both lists arranged in nondecreasing order, then \(r=s\) and \(p_i=q_i\) for all \(i\).
Proof sketch. Induct on \(r\). Since \(p_1\mid q_1\cdots q_s\), repeated application of Euclid's lemma gives \(p_1\mid q_j\) for some \(j\), hence \(p_1=q_j\) because both are prime. Since the lists are ordered and \(p_1\) is the smallest prime in the first list, one shows \(q_1=p_1\). Cancel this common factor and apply the inductive hypothesis to the remaining products. The base case \(r=1\) is immediate. \(\square\)

2. Congruences as an equivalence relation

Definition 9.4 For \(n>0\) and integers \(a,b\), \(a\equiv b\pmod n\) means \(n\mid(a-b)\).
Theorem 9.5 Congruence modulo \(n\) is an equivalence relation on \(\mathbb{Z}\); the equivalence classes are the residue classes \([0],[1],\ldots,[n-1]\).
Proof. Reflexivity: \(a-a=0=n\cdot0\). Symmetry: if \(a-b=nk\), then \(b-a=n(-k)\). Transitivity: if \(a-b=nk\) and \(b-c=n\ell\), then \(a-c=n(k+\ell)\). The division algorithm gives \(a=qn+r\) with \(0\le r<n\), so every class contains exactly one of \(0,1,\ldots,n-1\). \(\square\)

Arithmetic respects the relation: if \(a\equiv b\pmod n\) and \(c\equiv d\pmod n\), then \(a+c\equiv b+d\) and \(ac\equiv bd\pmod n\). These are the familiar sum and product rules; a proof was given in the number theory lecture notes. Consequently we may compute with residues, reducing after each operation.

3. Modular arithmetic and inverses

The set \(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}=\{[0],[1],\ldots,[n-1]\}\) with addition and multiplication of classes is a commutative ring. An element \([a]\) has a multiplicative inverse modulo \(n\) precisely when \(\gcd(a,n)=1\). In that case Bézout's identity gives \(ax+ny=1\), so \(ax\equiv1\pmod n\) and \([x]=[a]^{-1}\).

Example 9.6 Solve \(7x\equiv1\pmod{26}\). Running the Euclidean algorithm: \[26=3\cdot7+5,\quad7=1\cdot5+2,\quad5=2\cdot2+1,\] so \(1=5-2\cdot2=5-2(7-5)=3\cdot5-2\cdot7=3(26-3\cdot7)-2\cdot7=3\cdot26-11\cdot7\). Thus \(-11\cdot7\equiv1\pmod{26}\), so \(x\equiv-11\equiv15\pmod{26}\). Check: \(7\cdot15=105=4\cdot26+1\).

The Chinese remainder theorem in its simplest form says: if \(\gcd(m,n)=1\), the system \(x\equiv a\pmod m\), \(x\equiv b\pmod n\) has a solution, unique modulo \(mn\). A constructive solution uses \(u\) with \(um\equiv1\pmod n\) and \(v\) with \(vn\equiv1\pmod m\); then \[x=avn+bum\] satisfies both congruences. Existence follows from the inverses just constructed; uniqueness follows because two solutions differ by a multiple of both \(m\) and \(n\), hence of \(mn\).

4. Squares modulo small integers

Theorem 9.7
  1. Every perfect square is congruent to \(0\) or \(1\pmod4\).
  2. Every odd square is congruent to \(1\pmod8\).
  3. Every perfect square is congruent to \(0,1\), or \(4\pmod5\).
Proof of (3). Every integer is congruent modulo \(5\) to \(0,\pm1\), or \(\pm2\). Squaring the residues gives \(0,1,4,4,1\), so only \(0,1,4\) occur. The other parts are proved in Lecture 5, Problem 6 and Lecture 5, Problem 4 respectively. \(\square\)

Modular obstructions are a standard way to prove that equations have no integer solutions. For example, \(x^2+y^2=3\) has no integer solutions because squares modulo \(4\) are \(0\) or \(1\), so \(x^2+y^2\) modulo \(4\) can only be \(0,1,2\), never \(3\).

5. Fermat's little theorem

Theorem 9.8 (Fermat's little theorem) If \(p\) is prime and \(p\nmid a\), then \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\). Equivalently, \(a^p\equiv a\pmod p\) for every integer \(a\).
Proof via the binomial theorem. First prove \(p\mid\binom pk\) for \(1\le k\le p-1\): since \[\binom pk=\frac{p!}{k!(p-k)!},\] we have \(k!\binom pk=p(p-1)\cdots(p-k+1)\), so \(p\mid k!\binom pk\). Because \(p\) is prime and \(k<p\), \(\gcd(k!,p)=1\); hence \(p\mid\binom pk\).
Step 1. For \(a=1\), the congruence \(1^p\equiv1\pmod p\) is immediate.
Step 2. If \(a^p\equiv a\pmod p\), then by the binomial theorem \[(a+1)^p=\sum_{k=0}^p\binom pk a^k\equiv a^p+1\equiv a+1\pmod p,\] since all intermediate binomial coefficients are divisible by \(p\).
Step 3. Induction on nonnegative \(a\) gives \(a^p\equiv a\pmod p\) for all \(a\ge0\), and the result extends to negative \(a\) by writing \(a=-b\) and noting that \(p\) is odd, or by reducing \(a\) modulo \(p\).
Step 4. If \(p\nmid a\), multiply \(a^p\equiv a\pmod p\) by the inverse of \(a\) modulo \(p\) to obtain \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\). \(\square\)

Fermat's little theorem is indispensable for computations with large exponents. For example, \(3^{100}\pmod{101}\) is \(1\) because \(101\) is prime and \(101\nmid3\).

6. Extra detail: inverses and Fermat's little theorem

Theorem 9.10 If \(\gcd(a,n)=1\), then \(a\) has a multiplicative inverse modulo \(n\).
Proof. By Bezout's identity there are integers \(s,t\) with \(as+nt=1\). Reducing modulo \(n\) gives \(as\equiv1\pmod n\), so \(s\) is an inverse of \(a\) modulo \(n\). \(\square\)

The coprime condition is essential: \(2\) has no inverse modulo \(6\), because \(2x\) is always even modulo \(6\) and can never be congruent to \(1\). For a prime \(p\), every nonzero residue is coprime to \(p\), and Fermat's little theorem gives the inverse explicitly: \(a^{p-2}\) is an inverse of \(a\) modulo \(p\) because \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\).

Exercises
  1. Prove that if \(a\equiv b\pmod n\), then \(a^2\equiv b^2\pmod n\).
  2. Find the inverse of \(11\) modulo \(30\) using Bézout's identity.
  3. Solve \(4x\equiv6\pmod{10}\).
  4. Prove that \(n^2\equiv0\) or \(1\pmod3\) for every integer \(n\).
  5. Show that \(x^2-3y^2=2\) has no integer solutions by reducing modulo \(3\).
  6. Prove that if \(p\) is prime, then \(2^p\equiv2\pmod p\).
  7. Use the Chinese remainder theorem to solve \(x\equiv2\pmod3\), \(x\equiv3\pmod5\).
  8. Prove that if \(a\equiv b\pmod n\), then \(\gcd(a,n)=\gcd(b,n)\).
