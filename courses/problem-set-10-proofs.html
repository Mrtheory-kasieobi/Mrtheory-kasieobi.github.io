Problem Set 10: Combinatorial Proofs

Introduction to Proofs - Lecture 10 Medium

Try each problem before reading the solutions. A proof is judged by the completeness of its logical chain, not by how short it is. State your assumptions, justify every nontrivial step, and verify that the conclusion follows exactly.

Problems

Problem 1

Prove Pascal's identity \(\binom nk=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}k\) in two ways: combinatorially and algebraically.

Problem 2

Prove that a set with \(n\) elements has \(2^n\) subsets by induction, and explain how this relates to Theorem 10.5.

Problem 3

Prove that \(\sum_{k=0}^n k^2\binom nk=n(n+1)2^{n-2}\) for \(n\ge2\). (Hint: count triples \((S,a,b)\) with \(a,b\in S\subseteq X\).)

Problem 4

Prove that in any group of \(n\) people there are at least two people with the same number of acquaintances within the group.

Problem 5

Prove that \(\binom n0+\binom n1+\cdots+\binom nn=2^n\) using the binomial theorem, and compare with the combinatorial proof.

Problem 6

Prove that \(\binom{2n}{n}=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom{n}{k}^2\) by double counting.

Problem 7

Prove that \(\binom n2+\binom{n+1}2=n^2\) for \(n\ge1\) by a counting argument.

Problem 8

Use the pigeonhole principle to prove that every sequence of \(n^2+1\) distinct real numbers contains a subsequence of length \(n+1\) that is strictly increasing or strictly decreasing.

Problem 9

Prove that the number of ways to distribute \(n\) identical objects into \(k\) distinct boxes is \(\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}\).

Bonus Problem

Give a double-counting proof that \(\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2=\binom{2n}n\).
