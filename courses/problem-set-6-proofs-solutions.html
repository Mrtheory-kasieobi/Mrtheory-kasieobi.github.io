Solutions: Problem Set 6

Introduction to Proofs - Lecture 6

Key ideas used in these solutions

  • Proof by contradiction establishes \(q\) by showing that \( eg q\) is impossible.
  • The negation of \(p o q\) is \(p\land eg q\).
  • Irrationality proofs use a reduced fraction and force a common divisor.
  • Uniqueness proofs assume two objects and derive that they are equal.

Worked solutions

Problem 1: Prove by contradiction that there is no smallest positive rational number.

Suppose, for contradiction, that \(q>0\) is the smallest positive rational. Then \(q/2\) is rational and satisfies \(0<q/2<q\), contradicting the minimality of \(q\). Therefore no smallest positive rational exists.

Problem 2: Prove that \(\sqrt3\) is irrational.

Suppose \(\sqrt3=a/b\) with \(a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\), \(b\ne0\), and \(\gcd(a,b)=1\). Squaring gives \(a^2=3b^2\), so \(3\mid a^2\). Since \(3\) is prime, \(3\mid a\) (Euclid's lemma). Write \(a=3k\). Then \(9k^2=3b^2\), hence \(b^2=3k^2\), so \(3\mid b^2\) and therefore \(3\mid b\). Thus \(3\) divides both \(a\) and \(b\), contradicting \(\gcd(a,b)=1\). Hence \(\sqrt3\notin\mathbb{Q}\).

Problem 3: Prove by contradiction: if \(n\in\mathbb{Z}\) and \(n^2\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd.

Suppose \(n^2\) is odd but \(n\) is not odd, so \(n=2k\) for some integer \(k\). Then \(n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)\), which is even. This contradicts the assumption that \(n^2\) is odd. Hence \(n\) must be odd.

The same argument is a contrapositive proof: not odd means even, and even \(n\) gives even \(n^2\).

Problem 4: Prove that \(2+\sqrt3\) is irrational.

Suppose \(2+\sqrt3=r\in\mathbb{Q}\). Then \(\sqrt3=r-2\), which is rational because \(\mathbb{Q}\) is closed under subtraction. This contradicts the irrationality of \(\sqrt3\) proved above. Hence \(2+\sqrt3\) is irrational.

Problem 5: Prove by contradiction that if \(a,b\) are integers and \(a^2+b^2=0\), then \(a=b=0\).

Suppose \(a^2+b^2=0\) but \((a,b)\ne(0,0)\). Then \(a^2\ge0\) and \(b^2\ge0\), and their sum is \(0\), so \(a^2=0\) and \(b^2=0\). Hence \(a=0\) and \(b=0\), contradicting \((a,b)\ne(0,0)\). Thus \(a=b=0\).

This example shows that a contradiction proof can be quite short when the negation directly opposes a basic ordered-field fact.

Problem 6: Prove that there are infinitely many integers \(n\) for which \(n^2+1\) is not a perfect square.

Consider any integer \(n\ge1\). If \(n^2+1=m^2\) for some integer \(m\), then \(m^2-n^2=1\), so \((m-n)(m+n)=1\). Since \(m+n\ge1\), both factors are positive integers, so \(m-n=m+n=1\). Subtracting gives \(2n=0\), hence \(n=0\), contradicting \(n\ge1\). Therefore every positive integer \(n\) has \(n^2+1\) not a perfect square, so there are infinitely many such \(n\).

Problem 7: Prove by contradiction that \(\log_2 3\) is irrational.

Suppose \(\log_2 3=a/b\) with \(a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\), \(b>0\), in lowest terms. Then \(2^{a/b}=3\), so \(2^a=3^b\). The left side is even (for \(a\ge1\)) and the right side is odd. More rigor: since \(3^b>1\), \(a>0\), so \(2^a\) is even while \(3^b\) is odd, a contradiction. Hence \(\log_2 3\notin\mathbb{Q}\).

Problem 8: Analyse the following "proof": "To prove that \(n^2\) even implies \(n\) even, suppose \(n\) is even, \(n=2k\). Then \(n^2=4k^2\), which is even." Is it valid? If not, what method was actually used?

The argument proves "\(\:n\) even \(\implies n^2\) even", which is the converse of the desired statement. It is not a valid proof of the original implication. A correct contradiction proof assumes \(n^2\) is even and \(n\) is odd, then derives that \(n^2\) is odd. Equivalently, a contrapositive proof assumes \(n\) odd and shows \(n^2\) odd. The given argument starts from the wrong assumption.

Bonus Problem: Prove that there is no rational number whose square is \(3\).
Suppose \(x^2=3\) with \(x=a/b\) in lowest terms and \(b\ne0\). Then \(a^2=3b^2\), so \(3\mid a^2\), and since 3 is prime, \(3\mid a\). Write \(a=3k\). Then \(9k^2=3b^2\), so \(b^2=3k^2\), hence \(3\mid b\). Both \(a\) and \(b\) are divisible by 3, contradicting lowest terms.
