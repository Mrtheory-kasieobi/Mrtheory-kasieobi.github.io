Derivatives: Definition and Rules

Calculus I - Derivatives, Rules, Applications, and Linear Approximation (Detailed Notes)

Big picture

The derivative measures an instantaneous rate of change. It is born from a limit of ordinary secant slopes: as the two points on a curve merge, the secant line becomes the tangent line, and its slope becomes the derivative. From one definition we derive a small collection of rules that let us differentiate almost every function we meet, and we learn to interpret the results geometrically and physically.

Road map and prerequisites

Prerequisites: limits, limit laws, continuity, and the trigonometric limits \(\frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1\) and \(\frac{1 - \cos x}{x} \to 0\) from the previous note. We will cover:

  1. The limit definition; secant versus tangent lines
  2. Differentiability versus continuity; corners, cusps, and vertical tangents
  3. Constant, power, constant multiple, and sum/difference rules
  4. Product and quotient rules, proved without the chain rule
  5. Derivatives of sine, cosine, \(e^x\), and \(\ln x\), with derivations
  6. Notation, higher derivatives, tangent and normal lines, motion
  7. One-sided derivatives at endpoints and common mistakes
  8. Bonus: the derivative as best linear approximation

1. The definition of the derivative

1.1 From secant slopes to tangent slopes

For two distinct points \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) with \(x_1 \ne x_2\), the slope of the line through them is \(\frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\). On the graph of \(y = f(x)\), take the fixed point \((a, f(a))\) and a nearby point \((a + h, f(a + h))\) with \(h \ne 0\). The slope of this secant line is

\[ m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{(a + h) - a} = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}. \]

The condition \(h \ne 0\) is essential: the two points must be distinct for the slope formula to make sense, and the quotient is undefined at \(h = 0\). Letting \(h \to 0\) moves the second point toward the first, and the secant slopes tend to the tangent slope.

Definition (derivative at a point). The derivative of \(f\) at \(a\), denoted \(f'(a)\), is \[f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h},\] provided this limit exists as a finite number. Equivalently, \[f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}.\] If the limit exists, \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\); the number \(f'(a)\) is the slope of the tangent line at \((a, f(a))\).
Interpretations. Geometrically, \(f'(a)\) is the slope of the tangent line. Physically, if \(s(t)\) is position at time \(t\), then \(s'(t)\) is velocity and \(v'(t) = s''(t)\) is acceleration. In general the derivative is an instantaneous rate of change of the output with respect to the input.

1.2 First examples from the definition

Example 1.1 - Differentiating \(x^2\). Let \(f(x) = x^2\). Compute \(f'(a)\).
  1. Set up: \(f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(a + h)^2 - a^2}{h}\).
  2. Expand: \((a + h)^2 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2\), so the numerator becomes \(2ah + h^2\).
  3. Factor and cancel for \(h \ne 0\): \(\frac{h(2a + h)}{h} = 2a + h\).
  4. Take the limit: \(f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}(2a + h) = 2a\).
  5. Since \(a\) was arbitrary, \(f'(x) = 2x\) for all real \(x\).
Example 1.2 - Tangent line from the definition. For \(f(x) = x^2\), find the tangent line at \(x = 3\).
  1. The point is \((3, f(3)) = (3, 9)\).
  2. From Example 1.1, \(f'(3) = 2 \cdot 3 = 6\).
  3. Point-slope form: \(y - 9 = 6(x - 3)\), i.e. \(y = 6x - 9\).

2. Differentiability and continuity

2.1 Differentiability implies continuity

Theorem. If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\).
Proof. For \(h \ne 0\), write \[f(a + h) - f(a) = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \cdot h.\] Take limits as \(h \to 0\). The difference quotient tends to \(f'(a)\), and \(h\) tends to \(0\), so the product law for limits gives \[\lim_{h \to 0}\bigl(f(a + h) - f(a)\bigr) = f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0.\] Hence \(\lim_{h \to 0} f(a + h) = f(a)\), which is continuity at \(a\). Note that the identity was used only for \(h \ne 0\), exactly where the difference quotient is defined.
The converse is false. Continuity does not guarantee differentiability. A continuous graph can have a sharp corner, a cusp, or a vertical tangent. The next examples make this concrete.

2.2 Continuous but not differentiable

Example 2.1 - Corner: \(|x|\) at \(0\).
  1. \(|x|\) is continuous at \(0\) because \(\lim_{x \to 0} |x| = 0 = |0|\).
  2. Right-hand derivative: \(\lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|h| - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = 1\).
  3. Left-hand derivative: \(\lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{|h| - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{-h}{h} = -1\).
  4. The one-sided derivatives differ, so \(f'(0)\) does not exist. The graph has a corner at the origin.
Example 2.2 - Cusp: \(x^{2/3}\) at \(0\). \[ \frac{(0 + h)^{2/3} - 0}{h} = \frac{h^{2/3}}{h} = \frac{1}{h^{1/3}}. \]
  1. As \(h \to 0^+\), \(\frac{1}{h^{1/3}} \to +\infty\); as \(h \to 0^-\), it tends to \(-\infty\).
  2. The difference quotient is unbounded, so no finite derivative exists.
  3. The function is continuous at \(0\) but has a cusp there: the left and right branches become vertical in opposite directions.
Example 2.3 - Vertical tangent: \(x^{1/3}\) at \(0\). \[ \frac{(0 + h)^{1/3} - 0}{h} = \frac{1}{h^{2/3}} \to +\infty \quad \text{as } h \to 0. \]

The secant slopes grow without bound from both sides, so the derivative is not finite and \(f\) is not differentiable at \(0\). Geometrically the tangent line is vertical (\(x = 0\)); a vertical line has undefined slope, so this is not a finite derivative.

3. Basic differentiation rules

3.1 Constant rule

Theorem. If \(f(x) = c\) for all \(x\), then \(f'(x) = 0\).
For \(h \ne 0\), \(\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{c - c}{h} = 0\). The limit of the constant \(0\) is \(0\), so \(f'(x) = 0\).

3.2 Power rule: the honest statement

Theorem (power rule, integer exponents). If \(n\) is a positive integer, then \(\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^n\bigr] = n x^{n-1}\) for every real \(x\).
Proof for positive integers. By the binomial theorem, \((x + h)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} h^k\), so \((x + h)^n - x^n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} h^k\). For \(h \ne 0\), \[\frac{(x + h)^n - x^n}{h} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} h^{k-1}.\] As \(h \to 0\), every term with \(k \gt 1\) contains a positive power of \(h\) and vanishes, leaving only \(k = 1\): \(\binom{n}{1} x^{n-1} = n x^{n-1}\).
Scope of the proof. This argument proves the power rule only for positive integers \(n\). Negative integers require the reciprocal rule established in Section 4, rational exponents require a separate argument, and the formula \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^r] = r x^{r-1}\) for an arbitrary real exponent \(r\) is proved later by logarithmic differentiation. For \(r\) real, the formula is valid at least for \(x \gt 0\), where \(x^r = e^{r \ln x}\) is defined. Applying the binomial proof to non-integer exponents would be invalid, since the binomial theorem used here is a finite sum for integers only.
Example 3.1 - Using the power rule where it is justified.
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^3] = 3x^2\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^5] = 5x^4\).
  • The integer rule gives \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^{-2}] = -2x^{-3}\) for \(x \ne 0\) once the reciprocal rule is available (Section 4).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}[\sqrt{x}] = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{1/2}\bigr] = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\) for \(x \gt 0\); this will be justified with the general power rule later, and directly from the definition here:
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x + h} - \sqrt{x}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \quad (x \gt 0). \]

3.3 Constant multiple and sum/difference rules

Theorems. If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(x\) and \(c\) is a constant, then \[\frac{d}{dx}[c f(x)] = c f'(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[f(x) \pm g(x)] = f'(x) \pm g'(x).\]
Proofs. For the constant multiple rule, \[\frac{c f(x + h) - c f(x)}{h} = c \cdot \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \to c f'(x).\] For the sum rule, \[\frac{[f(x + h) + g(x + h)] - [f(x) + g(x)]}{h} = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} + \frac{g(x + h) - g(x)}{h} \to f'(x) + g'(x).\] The difference rule is identical with a minus sign.

3.4 Quick check

Quick check 1.
  1. Why must \(h\) be nonzero in \(\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\)? Otherwise we divide by zero and the two points coincide.
  2. Find \(\frac{d}{dx}[4x^3 - 2x + 7]\). \(12x^2 - 2\).
  3. Does continuity at a point imply differentiability there? No; \(|x|\) at \(0\) is the standard counterexample.

4. Product and quotient rules

4.1 Product rule

Theorem. If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(x\), then \[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(x) g(x)\bigr] = f'(x) g(x) + f(x) g'(x).\]
Proof. Add and subtract \(f(x + h) g(x)\) in the numerator: \[f(x + h)g(x + h) - f(x)g(x) = f(x + h)\bigl[g(x + h) - g(x)\bigr] + g(x)\bigl[f(x + h) - f(x)\bigr].\] Dividing by \(h \ne 0\) and taking limits, differentiability of \(f\) implies continuity of \(f\), so \(f(x + h) \to f(x)\). Hence \[\frac{d}{dx}[f g] = f(x) g'(x) + g(x) f'(x) = f'(x) g(x) + f(x) g'(x).\]
Example 4.1 - Product rule. Differentiate \(x^2 \sin x\).
  1. Choose \(f(x) = x^2\), \(g(x) = \sin x\), so \(f'(x) = 2x\) and \(g'(x) = \cos x\).
  2. Apply the rule: \(2x \cdot \sin x + x^2 \cdot \cos x\).
  3. Result: \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^2 \sin x] = 2x\sin x + x^2\cos x\).

4.2 Reciprocal derivative from the definition

Theorem (reciprocal rule). If \(g\) is differentiable at \(x\) and \(g(x) \ne 0\), then \(\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{g(x)}\right] = -\frac{g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2}\).
Proof. Since \(g(x) \ne 0\) and \(g\) is continuous at \(x\), we have \(g(x + h) \ne 0\) for all sufficiently small \(h\). For such \(h \ne 0\), \[\frac{\frac{1}{g(x + h)} - \frac{1}{g(x)}}{h} = \frac{g(x) - g(x + h)}{h\, g(x + h) g(x)} = -\frac{g(x + h) - g(x)}{h} \cdot \frac{1}{g(x + h) g(x)}.\] As \(h \to 0\), the first factor tends to \(g'(x)\) and the second to \(\frac{1}{[g(x)]^2}\). Hence the derivative is \(-\frac{g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2}\).

4.3 Quotient rule (proved without the chain rule)

Theorem. If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(x\) and \(g(x) \ne 0\), then \[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x) g(x) - f(x) g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2}.\]
Proof using the product and reciprocal rules. Write \(\frac{f}{g} = f \cdot \frac{1}{g}\). By the product rule and the reciprocal rule, \[\frac{d}{dx}\left[f \cdot \frac{1}{g}\right] = f' \cdot \frac{1}{g} + f \cdot \left(-\frac{g'}{g^2}\right) = \frac{f' g}{g^2} - \frac{f g'}{g^2} = \frac{f' g - f g'}{g^2}.\] This uses only results proved above; the chain rule is not needed. The condition \(g(x) \ne 0\) is required both for the quotient to be defined and for the reciprocal rule to apply.
Example 4.2 - Quotient rule. Differentiate \(\frac{x^2}{\sin x}\) where \(\sin x \ne 0\).
  1. Let \(f(x) = x^2\), \(g(x) = \sin x\), so \(f' = 2x\), \(g' = \cos x\).
  2. Apply the rule: \(\frac{2x \cdot \sin x - x^2 \cdot \cos x}{\sin^2 x}\).
  3. The result is valid only at points with \(\sin x \ne 0\).

4.4 Quick check

Quick check 2.
  1. Differentiate \(x \cos x\). \(\cos x - x \sin x\).
  2. Differentiate \(\frac{1}{x^3}\) for \(x \ne 0\) using the reciprocal rule. \(-\frac{3x^2}{x^6} = -3x^{-4}\).
  3. State the domain condition for the quotient rule. \(g(x) \ne 0\).

5. Derivatives of the basic transcendental functions

5.1 Sine and cosine

Theorem. \(\frac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \cos x\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}[\cos x] = -\sin x\), for angles in radians.
Derivation using the limits from the previous note. For \(h \ne 0\), \[\frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \frac{\sin x \cos h + \cos x \sin h - \sin x}{h} = \sin x \cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x \cdot \frac{\sin h}{h}.\] Using \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} = 0\) and \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = 1\), this tends to \(\cos x\). Similarly, \[\frac{\cos(x + h) - \cos x}{h} = \cos x \cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} - \sin x \cdot \frac{\sin h}{h} \to -\sin x.\] Both fundamental limits were proved earlier with the squeeze theorem, so these derivations are complete.

5.2 The natural exponential

Theorem. \(\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^x\bigr] = e^x\).
Derivation. \[ \frac{e^{x + h} - e^x}{h} = e^x \cdot \frac{e^h - 1}{h}. \] The number \(e\) is characterized by \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{e^h - 1}{h} = 1\) (equivalently, \(\lim_{u \to 0}(1 + u)^{1/u} = e\)); this defining limit is established when \(e\) is introduced. Therefore the derivative is \(e^x \cdot 1 = e^x\). For a general base \(a \gt 0\), writing \(a^x = e^{x \ln a}\) gives \(\frac{d}{dx}[a^x] = (\ln a)\, a^x\), once the chain rule is available.

5.3 The natural logarithm

Theorem. For \(x \gt 0\), \(\frac{d}{dx}[\ln x] = \frac{1}{x}\). More generally, for \(x \ne 0\), \(\frac{d}{dx}[\ln|x|] = \frac{1}{x}\).
Derivation. For \(x \gt 0\) and small \(h\), \[\frac{\ln(x + h) - \ln x}{h} = \frac{1}{h}\ln\left(\frac{x + h}{x}\right) = \frac{1}{h}\ln\left(1 + \frac{h}{x}\right) = \frac{1}{x}\cdot \frac{\ln(1 + u)}{u},\] where \(u = \frac{h}{x} \to 0\). Since \(\lim_{u \to 0}\frac{\ln(1 + u)}{u} = 1\) (the inverse of the defining limit for \(e\)), the derivative is \(\frac{1}{x}\). For \(x \lt 0\), \(\ln|x| = \ln(-x)\), and the same computation (or the chain rule, later) gives \(\frac{1}{x}\). Thus \(\frac{d}{dx}[\ln|x|] = \frac{1}{x}\) for all \(x \ne 0\), which is the correct extension of the logarithm's derivative.
Example 5.1 - Combining rules. Differentiate \(y = x^3 e^x - \ln|x|\).
  1. For \(x^3 e^x\), use the product rule: \(3x^2 e^x + x^3 e^x\).
  2. For \(\ln|x|\), the derivative is \(\frac{1}{x}\) when \(x \ne 0\).
  3. Therefore \(y' = 3x^2 e^x + x^3 e^x - \frac{1}{x}\), valid for \(x \ne 0\).

6. Notation

Common notations for the derivative.
  • \(f'(x)\) and \(y'\) are Lagrange notation; \(y'\) denotes the derivative of \(y\) with respect to the understood independent variable.
  • \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}f(x)\) are Leibniz notation, which records the variable of differentiation.
  • \(D f(x)\) is Euler notation using the operator \(D\).
  • \(\dot y\) is Newton notation, reserved for derivatives with respect to time, e.g. \(\dot s = \frac{ds}{dt}\).

In particular, \(y'\) is not Newton notation; the dot is Newton's mark. Mixing them up is a common error.

7. Higher derivatives and applications

7.1 Higher-order derivatives

Definition. If \(f'\) is itself differentiable, its derivative is the second derivative \(f''\). Continuing gives the \(n\)th derivative, written \(f^{(n)}\) or \(\frac{d^n f}{dx^n}\). By convention \(f^{(0)} = f\). The parentheses in \(f^{(n)}\) distinguish an \(n\)th derivative from a power \(f^n\).
Example 7.1 - Higher derivatives of a polynomial. Let \(f(x) = x^4 - 3x^2 + 2x\).
  1. \(f'(x) = 4x^3 - 6x + 2\).
  2. \(f''(x) = 12x^2 - 6\).
  3. \(f^{(3)}(x) = 24x\).
  4. \(f^{(4)}(x) = 24\), and \(f^{(5)}(x) = 0\).

7.2 Tangent and normal lines

Example 7.2 - Tangent and normal. For \(f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 1\) at \(x = 1\):
  1. Point: \(f(1) = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0\), so the point is \((1, 0)\).
  2. Slope: \(f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2\), so \(f'(1) = 1\).
  3. Tangent line: \(y - 0 = 1(x - 1)\), i.e. \(y = x - 1\).
  4. The normal line is perpendicular to the tangent, with slope \(-1/f'(1) = -1\), so \(y = -x + 1\). This requires \(f'(1) \ne 0\); if the tangent were horizontal the normal would be vertical.

7.3 Position, velocity, and acceleration

Example 7.3 - Motion along a line. A particle has position \(s(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t\) for \(t \ge 0\).
  1. Velocity: \(v(t) = s'(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 9\).
  2. Acceleration: \(a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t) = 6t - 12\).
  3. At \(t = 1\): \(v(1) = 3 - 12 + 9 = 0\), so the particle is momentarily at rest.
  4. Also \(a(1) = 6 - 12 = -6\), so its velocity is decreasing; it is about to move in the negative direction.

8. One-sided derivatives at endpoints

Definitions. If the domain contains a right neighborhood of \(a\), the right derivative is \(f'_+(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\). If it contains a left neighborhood of \(b\), the left derivative is \(f'_-(b) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(b + h) - f(b)}{h}\). Full differentiability at an interior point requires both one-sided derivatives to exist and agree.
Example 8.1 - Endpoint of \(\sqrt{x}\). At \(a = 0\), only the right derivative is available: \[f'_+(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^+}\frac{\sqrt{h} - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+}\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}} = +\infty.\]

No finite right derivative exists, so \(\sqrt{x}\) is not differentiable at the endpoint \(0\); the graph has a vertical tangent there. For \(x \gt 0\) the usual formula \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\) applies.

8.1 Common mistakes

  • Substituting \(h = 0\) into \(\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\); the quotient is defined only for \(h \ne 0\), and the limit is taken after simplifying.
  • Confusing \(f'(a)\), a number, with \(f'(x)\), a function of \(x\).
  • Assuming the power rule's binomial proof covers negative or fractional exponents; it does not.
  • Reversing the quotient rule's numerator: it is \(f'g - f g'\), not \(f g' - f'g\).
  • Writing \(y'\) as Newton notation; Newton notation is \(\dot y\), used for time.
  • Believing the product rule gives \(f'g'\); the correct form is \(f'g + fg'\).
  • Forgetting that differentiability implies continuity but not conversely, as \(|x|\) at \(0\) shows.

Bonus: the derivative as best linear approximation

Differentiability can be rephrased in a way that explains why the tangent line is useful for approximation.

Theorem. A function \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) with \(f'(a) = L\) if and only if there is a function \(\varepsilon(h)\) with \(\varepsilon(h) \to 0\) as \(h \to 0\) such that \[f(a + h) = f(a) + L h + h\,\varepsilon(h).\] Equivalently, \(f(a + h) = f(a) + L h + o(h)\), where \(o(h)\) denotes a quantity whose ratio to \(h\) tends to \(0\).
Proof. If \(f\) is differentiable, define for \(h \ne 0\) \[\varepsilon(h) = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} - L.\] Then \(\varepsilon(h) \to 0\) by definition of the derivative, and rearranging gives \(f(a + h) = f(a) + L h + h\varepsilon(h)\). Conversely, if such an \(\varepsilon\) exists, dividing by \(h \ne 0\) gives \(\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} = L + \varepsilon(h) \to L\), so \(f'(a) = L\).
Example Bonus.1 - Linear approximation. Approximate \(\sqrt{4.1}\). Take \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\), \(a = 4\), \(h = 0.1\). Since \(f(4) = 2\) and \(f'(4) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{4}\), \[\sqrt{4.1} \approx f(4) + f'(4)(0.1) = 2 + \frac{1}{4}(0.1) = 2.025.\] The error is of order \(h^2\), much smaller than the step \(h = 0.1\), because the remainder is \(o(h)\); indeed \((2.025)^2 \approx 4.1006\).

Summary

  • The derivative is the limit of difference quotients with \(h \ne 0\), giving the tangent slope and instantaneous rate of change.
  • Differentiability implies continuity, but corners, cusps, and vertical tangents show the converse fails.
  • Constant, integer-power, constant multiple, and sum/difference rules follow from limits; other exponents need later justification.
  • Product, reciprocal, and quotient rules are proved from the definition and each other, without the chain rule.
  • \((\sin x)' = \cos x\), \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\), \((e^x)' = e^x\), and \((\ln|x|)' = \frac{1}{x}\) (with domains as stated).
  • Higher derivatives, tangent and normal lines, and motion are direct applications; one-sided derivatives handle endpoints.

  4. Differentiate \(g(x) = x^3 \cos x\).

  1. Quotient rule: \(\frac{2(x^2 + 3) - (2x + 1)(2x)}{(x^2 + 3)^2} = \frac{2x^2 + 6 - 4x^2 - 2x}{(x^2 + 3)^2} = \frac{-2x^2 - 2x + 6}{(x^2 + 3)^2}\).
  2. \(p'(x) = e^x\sin x + e^x\cos x - \frac{1}{x} = e^x(\sin x + \cos x) - \frac{1}{x}\), for \(x \ne 0\).
  3. Point \((2, 6)\); \(f'(x) = 3x^2 - 1\), so \(f'(2) = 11\). Tangent: \(y - 6 = 11(x - 2)\), i.e. \(y = 11x - 16\). Normal slope \(-\frac{1}{11}\): \(y = -\frac{1}{11}(x - 2) + 6\).
  4. \(v(t) = 3t^2 - 6t\), \(a(t) = 6t - 6\). Setting \(v(t) = 3t(t - 2) = 0\) gives \(t = 0\) and \(t = 2\).
Answers 9-12.
  1. Write \(f(x) = x^{-1}\). Then \(f'(x) = -x^{-2}\), \(f''(x) = 2x^{-3}\), and \(f^{(3)}(x) = -6x^{-4}\), for \(x \ne 0\).
  2. \(f'_+(-1) = \lim_{h \to 0^+}\frac{\sqrt{(-1 + h) + 1} - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+}\frac{\sqrt{h}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+}\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}} = +\infty\), so no finite derivative exists at the endpoint.
  3. With \(a = 9\), \(f(9) = 3\) and \(f'(9) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{9}} = \frac{1}{6}\). Then \(\sqrt{9.2} \approx 3 + \frac{1}{6}(0.2) = 3 + 0.0333\ldots \approx 3.0333\), close to the true value about \(3.03315\).
  4. The binomial theorem used in the proof is a finite sum valid only for positive integer exponents. A non-integer exponent such as \(\pi\) requires the generalized power rule proved later via logarithmic differentiation, valid for \(x \gt 0\).
