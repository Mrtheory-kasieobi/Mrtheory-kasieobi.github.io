Lecture 5: Direct Proof and Proof by Contrapositive

Introduction to Proofs · Week 5

Lecture 5Direct Proof and Proof by Contrapositive

Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

  • Write a clean direct proof of an implication.
  • Use definitions of even, odd, divides, and congruence in algebraic proofs.
  • Prove implications by proving their contrapositives.
  • Prove biconditional statements by proving both directions.
  • Structure proofs by exhaustive cases and avoid common logical errors.
Beginner's bridge

A direct proof starts with the hypothesis and unfolds definitions until the conclusion appears. A contrapositive proof proves the equivalent statement \(\neg Q\to\neg P\). Use contrapositive when the negated conclusion is easier to work with than the original conclusion.

For example, to prove “if \(n^2\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd”, the contrapositive is “if \(n\) is even, then \(n^2\) is even”. The second statement is immediate by writing \(n=2k\), so the original follows automatically.

1. The shape of a direct proof

Most theorems in introductory mathematics are implications. To prove \(p\to q\) directly:

Step 1. State the assumption \(p\) ("Suppose \(p\) holds" or "Let \(a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\) satisfy the hypothesis").
Step 2. Unpack definitions. "\(a\) is even" means \(a=2k\) for some integer \(k\); "\(a\mid b\)" means \(b=ak\) for some integer \(k\); "\(a\equiv b\pmod n\)" means \(a-b=nk\) for some integer \(k\).
Step 3. Derive \(q\) using algebra, previously proved results, and valid logical steps.
Step 4. State the conclusion and mark the end of the proof.

The assumption and the conclusion must match the quantified statement exactly. If the theorem says "for every integer \(n\), if \(P(n)\) then \(Q(n)\)", begin with "Let \(n\in\mathbb{Z}\) and suppose \(P(n)\)"; prove \(Q(n)\) for this arbitrary \(n\).

Definition 5.1 (Even and odd) An integer \(n\) is even if \(n=2k\) for some \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\), and odd if \(n=2k+1\) for some \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\).

2. Worked direct proofs

Theorem 5.2 If \(m\) and \(n\) are even integers, then \(m+n\) is even.
Proof. Let \(m,n\) be even integers. By Definition 5.1 there exist \(a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\) with \(m=2a\) and \(n=2b\). Then \[m+n=2a+2b=2(a+b).\] Since \(a+b\in\mathbb{Z}\), the number \(m+n\) has the form \(2k\) with \(k=a+b\). Hence \(m+n\) is even. \(\square\)
Theorem 5.3 If \(a\mid b\) and \(b\mid c\), then \(a\mid c\).
Proof. Assume \(a\mid b\) and \(b\mid c\). By the definition of divisibility there exist integers \(r,s\) with \(b=ar\) and \(c=bs\). Substituting, \[c=bs=(ar)s=a(rs).\] Since \(rs\in\mathbb{Z}\), we conclude \(a\mid c\). \(\square\)

Notice the pattern: the conclusion is obtained by exhibiting the witness required by a definition. In Theorem 5.2 the witness is \(a+b\); in Theorem 5.3 it is \(rs\). A common error is to say "clearly \(m+n\) is even"; clarity is not a proof unless the algebraic witness is supplied.

Theorem 5.4 If \(n\in\mathbb{Z}\), then \(n^2+n\) is even.
Proof. Factor \(n^2+n=n(n+1)\). The integers \(n\) and \(n+1\) are consecutive, so exactly one of them is even (if \(n=2k\), then \(n\) is even; if \(n=2k+1\), then \(n+1=2(k+1)\) is even). In the first case \(n(n+1)=2k(n+1)\); in the second \(n(n+1)=n\cdot2(k+1)\). Either way the product is twice an integer, hence even. \(\square\)

3. Proof by contrapositive

From Lecture 1, \(p\to q\equiv\neg q\to\neg p\). Therefore to prove \(p\to q\) we may assume \(\neg q\) and derive \(\neg p\). This is often easier when the negation of the conclusion carries strong algebraic information.

Theorem 5.5 If \(n^2\) is even for an integer \(n\), then \(n\) is even.
Proof by contrapositive. We prove: if \(n\) is not even, then \(n^2\) is not even. Suppose \(n\) is odd, so \(n=2k+1\) for some \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\). Then \[n^2=(2k+1)^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1.\] Since \(2k^2+2k\in\mathbb{Z}\), \(n^2\) is odd, hence not even. Therefore \(n^2\) even implies \(n\) even. \(\square\)

Compare this with a direct proof: assuming \(n^2=2m\) does not immediately reveal that \(n\) is even, because extracting a square root of \(2m\) is not an integer operation. The contrapositive works with the tightly structured form \(2k+1\).

Theorem 5.6 Let \(n\in\mathbb{Z}\). If \(3n+2\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd.
Proof by contrapositive. Suppose \(n\) is even, say \(n=2k\). Then \(3n+2=6k+2=2(3k+1)\), which is even. Hence \(3n+2\) cannot be odd. By contraposition, if \(3n+2\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd. \(\square\)

Beware the converse error: the fact that \(n\) odd implies \(3n+2\) odd is the converse of Theorem 5.6, and it happens to be true here (\(3(2k+1)+2=6k+5=2(3k+2)+1\)), but it requires its own proof.

4. Biconditionals and chains

To prove \(p\leftrightarrow q\), prove \(p\to q\) and \(q\to p\). Often one direction is a direct proof and the other is best done by contrapositive.

Theorem 5.7 An integer \(n\) is even if and only if \(n^2\) is even.
Proof.
Forward (\(\Rightarrow\)). If \(n=2k\), then \(n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)\), so \(n^2\) is even.
Reverse (\(\Leftarrow\)). This is Theorem 5.5, proved by contrapositive.

Since both implications hold, \(n\) is even exactly when \(n^2\) is even. \(\square\)

For chains of implications, write \(p\to q\to r\) only when each arrow has been established; the transitivity of implication then gives \(p\to r\). Reversing one arrow invalidates the chain. In written work, label each implication and its justification rather than relying on the reader to infer direction.

5. Proof by cases

If the hypothesis guarantees \(p_1\lor p_2\lor\cdots\lor p_k\), and each case \(p_i\to q\) can be proved, then \(q\) follows by repeated use of the valid argument "proof by cases" from Lecture 1. The cases must be exhaustive: check that at least one applies in every situation.

Theorem 5.8 For every integer \(n\), \(n^2-n\) is even.
Proof. Let \(n\in\mathbb{Z}\). Every integer is either even or odd.
Case 1: \(n=2k\). Then \(n^2-n=4k^2-2k=2(2k^2-k)\), which is even.
Case 2: \(n=2k+1\). Then \(n^2-n=(2k+1)^2-(2k+1)=(2k+1)(2k)=2k(2k+1)\), which is even.

Since every integer falls into one of the two cases, the result holds for all integers. \(\square\)

Other useful case splits include: positive/zero/negative; congruence classes modulo \(m\); \(a<b\), \(a=b\), or \(a>b\) (trichotomy); and membership of an element in a union \(A\cup B\). Overlapping cases are allowed, but gaps are not.

6. Pitfalls

Four mistakes to avoid
  • Proof by example. Checking that a statement holds for \(n=1,2,3\) does not prove it for all \(n\).
  • Circular reasoning. The conclusion (or an equivalent form of it) may not appear among the assumptions.
  • Incorrect negation. The negation of "all \(A\) are \(B\)" is "some \(A\) is not \(B\)", not "no \(A\) is \(B\)".
  • Witness omissions. When a definition demands "there exists an integer \(k\)", explicitly identify that integer in terms of the given data.

Finally, ask whether the proof would still work if the hypothesis were weakened. If the argument uses more than the hypothesis provides, it is not a proof of the stated theorem.

6. Extra detail: biconditionals and cases

Theorem 5.10 To prove \(P\leftrightarrow Q\), it is enough to prove \(P\to Q\) and \(Q\to P\).
Proof. The formula \(P\leftrightarrow Q\) is true exactly when \(P\) and \(Q\) have the same truth value. The two implications rule out the two rows where the truth values differ, so together they force equality of truth values. \(\square\)
Example 5.10 To prove that \(n^2\) is even if and only if \(n\) is even, prove \(n\) even \(\Rightarrow n^2\) even directly by \(n=2k\), and prove \(n^2\) even \(\Rightarrow n\) even by contrapositive using \(n=2k+1\Rightarrow n^2=2(2k^2+2k)+1\).

Proof by cases is appropriate when the domain splits naturally, for example into even and odd integers. Make sure the cases exhaust every possibility; otherwise the proof covers only part of the domain.

Exercises
  1. Give a direct proof: if \(a,b\) are odd integers, then \(a+b\) is even.
  2. Give a direct proof: if \(a\mid b\) and \(c\mid d\), then \(ac\mid bd\).
  3. Prove by contrapositive: if \(5n+3\) is even, then \(n\) is odd.
  4. Prove: \(n\) is odd if and only if \(n^2\) is odd.
  5. Prove by cases: for every integer \(n\), \(n^3-n\) is divisible by \(3\).
  6. Find the error: "If \(n^2\) is divisible by \(4\), then \(n\) is even. Proof: let \(n=2k\); then \(n^2=4k^2\)."
  7. Prove that if \(a<b\) and \(c<d\), then \(a+c<b+d\).
  8. Prove or disprove: if \(a mid b\) and \(b mid c\), then \(a mid c\).
