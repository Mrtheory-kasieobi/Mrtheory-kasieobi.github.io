Solutions: Problem Set 4: Fermat's Little Theorem and Euler's Theorem

Elementary Number Theory Medium

Solutions

Problem 1

Solution: Compute \(3^6 = 729\) and \(729 \div 7 = 104\) remainder 1. So \(3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}\).

Problem 2

Solution: By Fermat's Little Theorem, \(2^{10} \equiv 1 \pmod{11}\). Thus \(2^{100} = (2^{10})^{10} \equiv 1^{10} = 1 \pmod{11}\).

Problem 3

Solution: By Fermat's Little Theorem: Since \(p\) is prime and \(p \nmid a\), we have \(a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\).

Problem 4

Solution: \(\phi(30) = \phi(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5) = 30 \cdot (1-\frac{1}{2})(1-\frac{1}{3})(1-\frac{1}{5}) = 30 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{4}{5} = 8\).

Problem 5

Solution: By Euler's theorem, \(5^{\phi(12)} = 5^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{12}\). Compute: \(5^2 = 25 \equiv 1 \pmod{12}\), so \(5^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{12}\).

Problem 6

Solution: \(\phi(100) = \phi(4 \cdot 25) = \phi(4) \cdot \phi(25) = 2 \cdot 20 = 40\). By Euler's theorem, \(3^{40} \equiv 1 \pmod{100}\). So \(3^{100} = (3^{40})^2 \cdot 3^{20} \equiv 3^{20} \pmod{100}\).

Problem 7

Solution: \(\phi(p) = |\{k : 1 \leq k \leq p-1, \gcd(k, p) = 1\}|\). Since \(p\) is prime, all integers \(1, 2, \ldots, p-1\) are coprime to \(p\). So \(\phi(p) = p-1\).

Problem 8

Solution: For \(n = 3\): \(\phi(3) = 2\). For \(n = 4\): \(\phi(4) = 2\). For \(n > 2\), if \(n\) has prime factor \(p\), then \(\phi(n) = n \prod(1-\frac{1}{p_i}) \geq n \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{p-1}{p} \geq 2\) for \(n \geq 4\).

Problem 9

Solution: By Fermat's Little Theorem, \(a^5 \equiv a \pmod{5}\) for all integers \(a\).

Problem 10

Solution: This statement is false. Counterexample: \(\phi(3) = 2\), \(\phi(6) = 2\), but \(2 \nmid 2\).

Bonus Problems

Bonus Problem 1

Solution:

Step 1: A Carmichael number is a composite number \(n\) such that \(a^n \equiv a \pmod{n}\) for all integers \(a\) with \(\gcd(a, n) = 1\).

Step 2: Verify 561 = 3 × 11 × 17 is a Carmichael number.

Step 3: Check: \(\phi(561) = 561 \cdot \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{10}{11} \cdot \frac{16}{17} = 320\).

Step 4: For any \(a\) with \(\gcd(a, 561) = 1\), we have \(a^{320} \equiv 1 \pmod{561}\), so \(a^{561} \equiv a \pmod{561}\).

Conclusion: 561 is indeed a Carmichael number.

Bonus Problem 2

Solution:

Step 1: In the multiplicative group \((\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*\) of order \(\phi(n)\), every element has order dividing \(\phi(n)\).

Step 2: By Lagrange's theorem, the order of any element divides the order of the group.

Step 3: Therefore, for any \(a\) coprime to \(n\), \(a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}\).

Conclusion: Euler's theorem follows from Lagrange's theorem.

Bonus Problem 3

Solution:

Step 1: Find the smallest \(k\) such that \(2^k \equiv 1 \pmod{37}\).

Step 2: By Fermat's Little Theorem, \(2^{36} \equiv 1 \pmod{37}\).

Step 3: The order of 2 divides 36. Check divisors: \(2^{18} = 262144 \equiv 36 \not\equiv 1 \pmod{37}\).

Step 4: Check \(2^9 = 512 \equiv 36 \pmod{37}\). Check \(2^{12} = 4096 \equiv 1 \pmod{37}\).

Conclusion: The smallest such \(k\) is 12.
