Lecture 3: Quadratic Functions

Mathematics 10 - Parabolas and Quadratic Equations

Introduction to Quadratic Functions

A quadratic function has the form \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\) with \(a \neq 0\). The highest power of \(x\) is 2, and its graph is a U-shaped curve called a parabola. The condition \(a \neq 0\) matters: if \(a = 0\), the \(x^2\) term disappears and the graph is just a line.

For example, \(f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3\) has \(a = 1\), \(b = -4\), and \(c = 3\). We will use it throughout these notes to connect the algebra to the picture.

Standard form of a quadratic equation: \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), with \(a \neq 0\). This is the function \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\) set equal to zero. Solving the equation means finding the \(x\)-values that make the expression zero.

Parabola, zeros, and intercepts in plain language: The graph is a U-shaped curve called a parabola. A zero (or root) is an \(x\)-value where \(f(x) = 0\). On the graph, zeros are the \(x\)-intercepts: the points where the curve crosses or touches the \(x\)-axis. A parabola may have two zeros, one zero, or none.

Standard Form and Vertex Form

Standard form: \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\). The coefficients \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are easy to read off.

Vertex form: \(f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k\), where \((h, k)\) is the vertex, the turning point of the graph.

In both forms, \(a\) controls which way the parabola opens and how wide it is.

Properties of Quadratic Functions

Vertex

The vertex is the turning point of the parabola: the lowest point if it opens up, or the highest point if it opens down. For \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\), the \(x\)-coordinate of the vertex is \(x = -\frac{b}{2a}\). To find the \(y\)-coordinate, substitute that \(x\) back into the function.

Reading the vertex: Think of the vertex as the tip of the U. In vertex form \(f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k\), it is simply \((h, k)\). In standard form, first find \(x = -\frac{b}{2a}\), then evaluate \(f(x)\).

Axis of Symmetry

A parabola is symmetric: its left and right sides are mirror images. The axis of symmetry is the vertical line \(x = -\frac{b}{2a}\) through the vertex. Points the same distance from this line have the same height.

Why it works: Points the same horizontal distance from \(x = -\frac{b}{2a}\) have equal \(y\)-values. That is why two zeros, when they exist, sit evenly on either side of the axis.

Direction

The sign of \(a\) decides the direction. If \(a > 0\), the parabola opens upward and the vertex is a minimum. If \(a < 0\), it opens downward and the vertex is a maximum.

Width from \(a\): The size of \(a\) controls how wide or narrow the parabola is. If \(|a| > 1\), the curve is narrower than \(y = x^2\) because the \(y\)-values grow faster. If \(0 < |a| < 1\), it is wider because the \(y\)-values grow more slowly. When \(a = 1\) or \(a = -1\), the width is the standard width.

Worked example: For \(f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3\), we have \(a = 1\) and \(b = -4\).

Vertex \(x\): \(x = -\frac{-4}{2(1)} = \frac{4}{2} = 2\)

Vertex \(y\): \(f(2) = 2^2 - 4(2) + 3 = 4 - 8 + 3 = -1\), so the vertex is \((2, -1)\)

The axis of symmetry is \(x = 2\), and since \(a = 1 > 0\), the parabola opens upward with a minimum at the vertex.

Worked Table for \(f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3\)

Choose \(x\)-values around \(x = 2\), the axis of symmetry, and compute each output.

\(x = 0\): \(f(0) = 0 - 0 + 3 = 3\)

\(x = 1\): \(f(1) = 1 - 4 + 3 = 0\)

\(x = 2\): \(f(2) = 4 - 8 + 3 = -1\)

\(x = 3\): \(f(3) = 9 - 12 + 3 = 0\)

\(x = 4\): \(f(4) = 16 - 16 + 3 = 3\)

The outputs \(3, 0, -1, 0, 3\) are symmetric about \(x = 2\).

The smallest output is \(-1\) at \(x = 2\), so the vertex is \((2, -1)\) and the axis of symmetry is \(x = 2\).

Where \(f(x) = 0\) we get \(x = 1\) and \(x = 3\), so the zeros are \(1\) and \(3\), with intercepts \((1, 0)\) and \((3, 0)\).

Factoring Quadratic Expressions

Factoring reverses expansion. We rewrite \(ax^2 + bx + c\) as a product of two simpler expressions, which makes the zeros easy to see.

Common factoring patterns:

  • \(a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)\) - Difference of squares
  • \(a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = (a + b)^2\) - Perfect square trinomial
  • \(ax^2 + bx + c\) - Factor by finding two numbers that multiply to \(ac\) and add to \(b\)

Factored form and zeros: When \(f(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\), the numbers \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) are the zeros: the \(x\)-values where \(f(x) = 0\). These are exactly the \(x\)-intercepts of the graph.

For our example, \(x^2 - 4x + 3\) factors as \((x - 1)(x - 3)\), so \(f(x) = (x - 1)(x - 3)\). Its zeros are \(x = 1\) and \(x = 3\), giving intercepts \((1, 0)\) and \((3, 0)\). Notice that 1 and 3 sit evenly on either side of the axis \(x = 2\).

Sign Logic for \(x^2 + bx + c\)

We need numbers \(m\) and \(n\) with \(mn = c\) and \(m + n = b\). The signs of \(b\) and \(c\) tell us what to try.

  • If \(c > 0\), both numbers share a sign: positive when \(b > 0\), negative when \(b < 0\).
  • If \(c < 0\), the signs are opposite, and the larger number takes the sign of \(b\).
  • Always check by expanding your factors back to the original expression.
Solving Quadratic Equations by Factoring

To solve \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) by factoring:

  1. Factor the quadratic expression
  2. Set each factor equal to zero
  3. Solve the small equation for \(x\)

Example: \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\) becomes \((x - 2)(x - 3) = 0\), so \(x = 2\) or \(x = 3\)

You can always check by expanding: \((x - 2)(x - 3) = x^2 - 5x + 6\) ✓

Worked Example: \(x^2 - 5x + 6\)

Here \(b = -5\) and \(c = 6\). Since \(c > 0\) and \(b < 0\), both numbers are negative.

We need two negative numbers with product \(6\) and sum \(-5\): try \(-2\) and \(-3\), since \((-2)(-3) = 6\) and \(-2 + (-3) = -5\).

So \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)\).

Set each factor to zero: \(x - 2 = 0\) gives \(x = 2\), and \(x - 3 = 0\) gives \(x = 3\).

The zeros are \(2\) and \(3\), so the graph crosses the \(x\)-axis at \((2, 0)\) and \((3, 0)\).

Check: \((x - 2)(x - 3) = x^2 - 3x - 2x + 6 = x^2 - 5x + 6\) ✓

Quadratic Formula

Factoring is fast when it works, but many quadratics do not factor nicely. The quadratic formula solves \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) for any \(a \neq 0\):

\[x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\]

The \(\pm\) means there are usually two answers: one using \(+\) and one using \(-\).

Example: solve \(x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0\), where \(a = 1\), \(b = -4\), and \(c = 3\)

\(x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(1)(3)}}{2(1)}\)

Inside the square root: \(16 - 12 = 4\), so \(\sqrt{4} = 2\)

Using \(+\): \(x = \frac{4 + 2}{2} = 3\)

Using \(-\): \(x = \frac{4 - 2}{2} = 1\)

These are the same zeros we found by factoring. The formula always works; factoring is simply quicker when it is possible.

Another worked example: Solve \(2x^2 - 3x - 2 = 0\), where \(a = 2\), \(b = -3\), and \(c = -2\)

Discriminant: \(b^2 - 4ac = (-3)^2 - 4(2)(-2) = 9 + 16 = 25\)

Since \(\sqrt{25} = 5\), substitute into the formula:

\[x = \frac{-(-3) \pm 5}{2(2)} = \frac{3 \pm 5}{4}\]

Using \(+\): \(x = \frac{3 + 5}{4} = \frac{8}{4} = 2\)

Using \(-\): \(x = \frac{3 - 5}{4} = \frac{-2}{4} = -\frac{1}{2}\)

The solutions are \(x = 2\) or \(x = -\frac{1}{2}\). Check \(x = 2\): \(2(4) - 3(2) - 2 = 8 - 6 - 2 = 0\) ✓

Check \(x = -\frac{1}{2}\): \(2\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) - 3\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) - 2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{2} - 2 = 0\) ✓

Exploring Quadratic Coefficients

Changing the coefficients affects the shape and position of a parabola. Change one coefficient at a time: \(a\) makes the graph wider, narrower, or flips it; \(c\) slides it up or down; and \(b\) shifts the vertex sideways. The vertex, axis of symmetry, and zeros change with the coefficients.

Beginner summary:

  • \(a\) controls opening and width: \(a > 0\) opens up, \(a < 0\) opens down. Larger \(|a|\) makes it narrower; smaller \(|a|\) makes it wider.
  • \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept: since \(f(0) = c\), the curve always passes through \((0, c)\).
  • \(b\) helps shift the vertex: the axis is \(x = -\frac{b}{2a}\), so changing \(b\) moves the vertex left or right.

Boundary examples: For \(y = x^2\) we have \(a = 1\), \(b = 0\), \(c = 0\): it opens up with vertex \((0, 0)\) and passes through the origin. For \(y = -x^2\), \(a = -1\) flips it downward with the same width. For \(y = x^2 + 3\), \(c = 3\) moves the \(y\)-intercept to \((0, 3)\). For \(y = x^2 - 4x\), \(c = 0\), so one zero is \(x = 0\).

Check Your Understanding

Work on a separate page first, then compare your answers below.

  1. Find the vertex and axis of symmetry of \(f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 5\).
  2. Solve \(x^2 + x - 6 = 0\) by factoring.
  3. Use the quadratic formula to solve \(x^2 - 4x + 4 = 0\).

Worked answers:

  1. With \(a = 1\), \(b = -6\): \(x = -\frac{-6}{2} = 3\) and \(f(3) = 9 - 18 + 5 = -4\). The vertex is \((3, -4)\); the axis is \(x = 3\).
  2. Numbers with product \(-6\) and sum \(1\) are \(3\) and \(-2\). So \((x + 3)(x - 2) = 0\), giving \(x = -3\) or \(x = 2\).
  3. With \(a = 1\), \(b = -4\), \(c = 4\): the discriminant is \(16 - 16 = 0\), so \(x = \frac{4 \pm 0}{2} = 2\). There is one repeated root, \(x = 2\).

Common Mistakes:

  • Sign errors in the formula, such as using \(b\) instead of \(-b\) when \(b\) is negative
  • Forgetting the two roots: the \(\pm\) gives both answers unless the discriminant is zero
  • Confusing \(c\) with an \(x\)-intercept: \(c\) gives the \(y\)-intercept \((0, c)\), not where \(y = 0\)
  • Treating \(x^2\) like \(2x\): \(x^2\) means \(x \times x\), not \(2x\)

Bonus: Reading the Discriminant

The Part Under the Square Root

In the quadratic formula, \(b^2 - 4ac\) is called the discriminant. When it is \(\geq 0\), the zeros are real numbers.

If it is positive there are two \(x\)-intercepts; if it is zero the vertex just touches the \(x\)-axis once.

If it is negative the graph never crosses the \(x\)-axis.
