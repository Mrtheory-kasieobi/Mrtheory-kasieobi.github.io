Lecture 6: Linear Functions

Mathematics 10 - Chapter 6

6.1 Representing Linear Functions

A linear function has the form \(f(x) = mx + b\) where \(m\) is the slope and \(b\) is the y-intercept. The graph is a straight line.

Why Linear Functions Are Important: Linear functions model situations with constant rate of change. They're used in economics for supply and demand, in physics for uniform motion, and in everyday life for calculating costs, distances, and simple interest.

Linear Function Forms

Slope-Intercept Form: \(y = mx + b\)

Point-Slope Form: \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\)

Standard Form: \(Ax + By = C\)

where \(m\) is the slope and \(b\) is the y-intercept.

Example 1: Identifying Linear Functions

Which of these are linear? \(f(x) = 3x - 2\), \(g(x) = x^2 + 1\), \(h(x) = \frac{1}{x} + 5\)

Only \(f(x) = 3x - 2\) is linear. The others have variables with exponents other than 1.

Motivation: Linear functions are the foundation for understanding more complex functions and for linear approximation in calculus.

6.2 Interpreting Slope and Intercepts

The slope represents the rate of change. A positive slope means increasing, negative means decreasing. The y-intercept is the starting value when \(x = 0\).

Real-World Meaning: If \(C(t) = 50t + 1000\) represents the cost of production after \(t\) items, the slope 50 is the cost per item, and 1000 is the fixed cost.

Slope Formula

\(m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}\)

Interpretation: For each unit increase in \(x\), \(y\) changes by \(m\) units.

6.3 Linear Interpolation and Extrapolation

Interpolation estimates values between known data points. Extrapolation estimates values beyond the known data.

Important Note: Extrapolation can be dangerous! Just because data follows a linear pattern in one range doesn't mean it will continue that way. Population growth looks linear for a short time but eventually becomes exponential.

Example 2: Linear Interpolation

A car travels 60 km in 1 hour and 120 km in 2 hours. Estimate the distance at 1.5 hours.

Using the linear model: \(d = 60t\), at \(t = 1.5\): \(d = 90\) km

Motivation: This is used in science to estimate values between measurements and in business to predict sales.

6.4 Solving Linear Function Equations

To solve a linear equation, isolate the variable. The goal is to get \(x\) alone on one side.

Systematic Approach:

  1. Collect like terms on each side
  2. Move the variable term to one side
  3. Move the constant term to the other side
  4. Divide by the coefficient of the variable
Example 3: Solving a Linear Equation

Solve: \(3(x - 2) + 5 = 2x + 10\)

\(3x - 6 + 5 = 2x + 10\)

\(3x - 1 = 2x + 10\)

\(x = 11\)

Motivation: This is used to find break-even points, solve for unknown quantities, and find intersection points of lines.

6.5 Linear Inequalities

Linear inequalities are solved like equations, but the inequality sign flips when we multiply or divide by a negative number.

Real-World Application: If you need to sell at least 50 items to break even, and you sell 5 per day, you need \(5d \geq 50\), so \(d \geq 10\) days.

Inequality Symbols

\(a < b\): a is less than b

\(a \leq b\): a is less than or equal to b

\(a > b\): a is greater than b

\(a \geq b\): a is greater than or equal to b

6.6 Graphing Linear Inequalities

To graph a linear inequality:

  1. Graph the corresponding equation as a boundary line
  2. Use a solid line for \(\leq\) or \(\geq\), dashed for \(<\) or \(>\)
  3. Test a point not on the line to determine which side to shade
Example 4: Graphing an Inequality

Graph \(2x + 3y < 6\)

Boundary line: \(2x + 3y = 6\) or \(y = -\frac{2}{3}x + 2\)

Test point (0,0): \(2(0) + 3(0) = 0 < 6\) ✓

Shade the side containing (0,0) with a dashed line.

Motivation: This is used in optimization problems, business constraints, and engineering design limits.

6.7 Linear Function Applications

Linear functions model many real-world situations with constant rates of change.

Common Applications:

  • Cost functions: \(C(x) = \text{fixed cost} + (\text{cost per item}) \times x\)
  • Distance-speed-time: \(d = vt\) (constant speed)
  • Simple interest: \(I = P \times r \times t\)
  • Temperature conversion: \(C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32)\)
