Solutions: Problem Set 9: Gaussian Primes

Solutions

Problem 1

Solution: Gaussian primes with norm < 10: \(1+i\) (norm 2), \(2+i, 2-i\) (norm 5), and their associates \(-1+i, -2-i, -2+i, 1-i\).

Problem 2

Solution: In \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\): primes \(p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}\) remain prime (inert). Primes \(p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\) split as \(p = (a+bi)(a-bi)\). \(p = 2\) ramifies: \(2 = -i(1+i)^2\).

Problem 3

Solution: \(5 = (2+i)(2-i)\). Both factors have norm 5, which is prime in \(\mathbb{Z}\), so both are Gaussian primes.

Problem 4

Solution: \(7 \equiv 3 \pmod{4}\), so 7 is inert and remains prime in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\). There is no factorization \(7 = \alpha\beta\) with non-units \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{Z}[i]\).

Problem 5

Solution: If \(p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}\), then \(-1\) is not a quadratic residue mod \(p\). So \(x^2+1 \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\) has no solution, meaning \(p\) cannot be written as \(a^2+b^2\), hence remains prime in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\).

Problem 6

Solution: \(13 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\), so 13 splits. Find \(a, b\) with \(a^2+b^2 = 13\): \(2^2 + 3^2 = 13\). So \(13 = (2+3i)(2-3i)\). Both factors are Gaussian primes.

Problem 7

Solution: If \(p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\), then \(\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right) = 1\), so \(-1\) is a quadratic residue mod \(p\). Thus \(x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{p}\) has a solution \(a\), meaning \(p | (a^2+1) = (a+i)(a-i)\) in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\). Hence \(p\) splits.

Problem 8

Solution: \(65 = 5 \cdot 13 = (2+i)(2-i)(3+2i)(3-2i)\). This is the complete factorization into Gaussian primes.

Problem 9

Solution: \(50 = 2 \cdot 5^2 = (1+i)(1-i) \cdot 5^2\). In Gaussian integers: \(50 = (1+i)(1-i)(2+3i)^2(2-3i)^2\) or more simply \(50 = (1+i)(1-i) \cdot 5^2\).

Problem 10

Solution: \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\) is a Euclidean domain, hence a UFD. Unique factorization means every non-zero non-unit has a factorization into irreducibles, unique up to order and associates (units: \(1, -1, i, -i\)).

Bonus Problems

Bonus Problem 1

Solution:

Step 1: For prime \(p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\), we can write \(p = a^2 + b^2\) using Fermat's theorem on sums of two squares.

Step 2: Find \(k\) with \(k^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{p}\) using Tonelli-Shanks algorithm.

Step 3: Then \(p | (k^2+1)\), so in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\), \(p | (k+i)(k-i)\).

Step 4: Since \(p\) doesn't divide either factor, \(p = (k+i)(k-i)\) up to units.

Bonus Problem 2

Solution:

Step 1: Suppose \(p = a^2+b^2 = c^2+d^2\) with \(a,b,c,d > 0\) and \((a,b) \neq (c,d), (d,c)\).

Step 2: In \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\): \(p = (a+bi)(a-bi) = (c+di)(c-di)\).

Step 3: By unique factorization in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\), \(a+bi\) and \(c+di\) are associates.

Step 4: This means \((a,b) = \pm(c,d)\) or \((a,b) = \pm(d,c)\), giving essentially the same representation.

Bonus Problem 3

Solution:

Step 1: \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\) is Euclidean: for any \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{Z}[i]\) with \(\beta \neq 0\), there exist \(\gamma, \delta\) with \(\alpha = \beta\gamma + \delta\) and \(N(\delta) < N(\beta)\).

Step 2: Euclidean domains are UFDs by standard ring theory.

Step 3: The Euclidean function \(N\) is multiplicative on units and respects division with remainder.
