Solutions: Problem Set 9

Introduction to Proofs - Lecture 9

Key ideas used in these solutions

  • Congruence \(a\equiv b\pmod n\) means \(a-b\) is a multiple of \(n\).
  • Inverses modulo \(n\) exist exactly for residues coprime to \(n\).
  • Squares have restricted residues; these restrictions prove nonexistence results.
  • Fermat's little theorem follows from divisibility of intermediate binomial coefficients.

Worked solutions

Problem 1: Prove that if \(a\equiv b\pmod n\) and \(c\equiv d\pmod n\), then \(ac\equiv bd\pmod n\).

Write \(a=b+kn\) and \(c=d+\ell n\) with \(k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}\). Then \[ac=(b+kn)(d+\ell n)=bd+b\ell n+dk n+k\ell n^2=bd+n(b\ell+dk+k\ell n).\] Since the bracket is an integer, \(ac-bd\) is a multiple of \(n\), so \(ac\equiv bd\pmod n\).

Problem 2: Solve \(6x\equiv9\pmod{15}\) completely.
Solvability. \(\gcd(6,15)=3\) and \(3\mid9\), so solutions exist. Dividing through by \(3\) gives the equivalent congruence \(2x\equiv3\pmod5\).
Inverse. \(2^{-1}\equiv3\pmod5\) since \(2\cdot3=6\equiv1\pmod5\). Multiplying gives \(x\equiv9\equiv4\pmod5\).
Lift back. The solutions modulo \(15\) are \(x\equiv4,9,14\pmod{15}\), i.e. \(x=4+5t\) with \(t=0,1,2\). There are exactly \(\gcd(6,15)=3\) incongruent solutions modulo \(15\).
Problem 3: Prove that \(5 mid(n^2+2)\) for every integer \(n\).

By Theorem 9.7, \(n^2\equiv0,1,4\pmod5\). Hence \(n^2+2\equiv2,3,1\pmod5\), never \(0\). Therefore \(5\nmid(n^2+2)\) for any integer \(n\).

Problem 4: Prove that every prime \(p>3\) satisfies \(p^2\equiv1\pmod{12}\).

Since \(p>3\) is prime, \(p\) is odd, so \(p^2\equiv1\pmod8\), hence certainly \(p^2\equiv1\pmod4\). Also \(3\nmid p\), so \(p\equiv\pm1\pmod3\), giving \(p^2\equiv1\pmod3\). Since \(3\) and \(4\) are coprime, \(p^2-1\) is divisible by \(12\), i.e. \(p^2\equiv1\pmod{12}\).

Problem 5: Use the Chinese remainder theorem to solve \(x\equiv1\pmod4\), \(x\equiv2\pmod7\).

Since \(\gcd(4,7)=1\), there is a unique solution modulo \(28\). We need \(u\) with \(4u\equiv1\pmod7\): \(u=2\) works since \(8\equiv1\). We need \(v\) with \(7v\equiv1\pmod4\): \(v=3\) works since \(21\equiv1\). Take \[x=1\cdot7\cdot3+2\cdot4\cdot2=21+16=37\equiv9\pmod{28}.\] Check: \(9\equiv1\pmod4\) and \(9\equiv2\pmod7\).

Problem 6: Prove that if \(p\) is prime and \(a^2\equiv1\pmod p\), then \(a\equiv1\) or \(a\equiv-1\pmod p\).

From \(a^2\equiv1\pmod p\) we get \(p\mid(a^2-1)=(a-1)(a+1)\). By Euclid's lemma, \(p\mid(a-1)\) or \(p\mid(a+1)\). Hence \(a\equiv1\pmod p\) or \(a\equiv-1\pmod p\). This can fail modulo composite numbers: \(3^2\equiv1\pmod8\) but \(3\not\equiv\pm1\pmod8\).

Problem 7: Compute \(7^{222}\) modulo \(13\) using Fermat's little theorem.

Since \(13\) is prime and \(13\nmid7\), Fermat gives \(7^{12}\equiv1\pmod{13}\). Divide the exponent: \(222=12\cdot18+6\), so \(7^{222}\equiv(7^{12})^{18}\cdot7^6\equiv7^6\pmod{13}\). Now \(7^2=49\equiv10\), \(7^3\equiv10\cdot7=70\equiv5\), and \(7^6\equiv5^2=25\equiv12\equiv-1\pmod{13}\). Thus \(7^{222}\equiv12\pmod{13}\).

Problem 8: Prove that there are infinitely many primes congruent to \(3\pmod4\).

Suppose there are finitely many, \(p_1,\ldots,p_k\). Let \(N=4p_1p_2\cdots p_k-1\). Then \(N\equiv3\pmod4\), so in the prime factorisation of \(N\) there must be at least one prime \(q\equiv3\pmod4\); otherwise all prime factors would be \(1\pmod4\) (or \(2\), impossible since \(N\) is odd), making \(N\equiv1\pmod4\). But \(q\) cannot be any \(p_i\), because \(N\equiv-1\pmod{p_i}\). This contradicts the assumed completeness of the list, so infinitely many primes are \(3\pmod4\).

Bonus Problem: Prove that if \(x\) is odd, then \(x^2\equiv1\pmod8\).
Write \(x=2k+1\). Then \(x^2=4k^2+4k+1=4k(k+1)+1\). Since \(k\) and \(k+1\) are consecutive, one is even, so \(k(k+1)\) is even. Hence \(4k(k+1)\) is divisible by \(8\), and \(x^2\equiv1\pmod8\).
