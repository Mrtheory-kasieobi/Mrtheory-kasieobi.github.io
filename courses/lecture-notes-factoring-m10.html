Lecture 3: Factors and Products

Mathematics 10 - Chapter 3

3.1 Factors and Multiples of Whole Numbers

A factor of a number divides it evenly, with no remainder. For example, \(4\) is a factor of \(20\) because \(20 \div 4 = 5\). A multiple is what you get when you multiply a number by a whole number: \(20\) is a multiple of \(4\) because \(4 \times 5 = 20\).

Why This Matters: Factors tell us how to split things into equal groups. If you have 36 chairs, the factors of 36 give every equal-row arrangement: 1 row of 36, 2 rows of 18, 3 rows of 12, 4 rows of 9, or 6 rows of 6.

Prime Factorization

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 with exactly two factors, 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, and 7 are primes.

A composite number is a whole number greater than 1 with more than two factors, such as 4, 6, 9, and 12.

The number 1 is neither prime nor composite, because it has only one factor: itself.

Every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way.

Example: \(60 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 = 2^{2} \times 3 \times 5\).

Divide by the smallest prime that works at each step: \(60 \div 2 = 30\), then \(30 \div 2 = 15\), then \(15 \div 3 = 5\).

Application: Prime factorization makes it easy to find the greatest common factor and the lowest common multiple of two numbers.

Example 1: Finding All Factors

Find all factors of 36.

A simple method: test divisors in order, 1, 2, 3, and so on, and stop when you reach the square root of the number.

Here \(\sqrt{36} = 6\), so test only 1 through 6. Each divisor that divides evenly gives one factor pair.

Start with the prime factorization: \(36 = 2^{2} \times 3^{2}\).

List the factor pairs: \(1 \times 36\), \(2 \times 18\), \(3 \times 12\), \(4 \times 9\), \(6 \times 6\). (5 does not divide 36 evenly.)

We stop at \(6 \times 6 = 36\), because any later pair would repeat a pair already found.

Factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. Check by dividing 36 by each one.

Example: Finding the Greatest Common Factor

Find the greatest common factor (GCF) of 24 and 36.

Write each prime factorization: \(24 = 2^{3} \times 3\) and \(36 = 2^{2} \times 3^{2}\).

Take the smallest power of each shared prime: \(2^{2} \times 3 = 12\).

The GCF is 12. Check: \(24 = 12 \times 2\) and \(36 = 12 \times 3\), and 2 and 3 share no further factor.

3.2 Factoring Polynomials

To factor a polynomial means to write it as a product of simpler polynomials. Factoring is reverse expanding: expanding \((x + 3)(x + 4)\) gives \(x^{2} + 7x + 12\), so factoring takes \(x^{2} + 7x + 12\) back to \((x + 3)(x + 4)\).

Why Factor Polynomials? Factoring helps us solve equations and simplify expressions. When a quadratic is factored, its zeros are easy to find, and those zeros tell us where the graph crosses the x-axis.

Common Factoring Patterns

Common Factor: \(ax + ay = a(x + y)\). Always check for a common factor first.

Difference of Squares: \(a^{2} - b^{2} = (a - b)(a + b)\)

Trinomial (\(a = 1\)): \(x^{2} + bx + c = (x + m)(x + n)\) when \(m \times n = c\) and \(m + n = b\).

Check: Expanding your answer must give back the original expression.

Example: Taking Out a Greatest Common Factor First

Factor \(6x + 9\).

The coefficients 6 and 9 share a greatest common factor of 3, and there is no common variable.

Divide each term by 3: \(6x \div 3 = 2x\) and \(9 \div 3 = 3\).

So \(6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)\).

Check by expanding: \(3(2x + 3) = 6x + 9\).

First Step: Always check for a common factor before trying another factoring pattern.

Sign Rules for \(x^{2} + bx + c\)

We want numbers \(m\) and \(n\) with \(m n = c\) and \(m + n = b\). The signs of \(c\) and \(b\) tell us which signs to try.

  • If \(c > 0\), the two signs are the same: both positive when \(b > 0\), and both negative when \(b < 0\).
  • If \(c < 0\), the signs are opposite, and the number with the larger absolute value takes the sign of \(b\).

Example: For \(x^{2} - x - 12\), \(c < 0\) and \(b < 0\). The opposite-sign pair is \(-4\) and \(3\), so \(x^{2} - x - 12 = (x - 4)(x + 3)\).

Example 2: Factoring a Trinomial

Factor \(x^{2} + 7x + 12\).

We need two numbers that multiply to \(12\) and add to \(7\). Try the factor pairs of 12: \(1 + 12 = 13\), \(2 + 6 = 8\), and \(3 + 4 = 7\).

So \(x^{2} + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)\).

Check by expanding: \((x + 3)(x + 4) = x^{2} + 4x + 3x + 12 = x^{2} + 7x + 12\).

Zero-Product Property: If \(ab = 0\), then \(a = 0\) or \(b = 0\).

To solve \(x^{2} + 7x + 12 = 0\), write \((x + 3)(x + 4) = 0\), so \(x + 3 = 0\) or \(x + 4 = 0\). This gives \(x = -3\) or \(x = -4\).

3.3 Factoring Quadratic Expressions

A quadratic expression has the form \(ax^{2} + bx + c\). When \(a \neq 1\), find two numbers that multiply to \(ac\) and add to \(b\), split the middle term using those numbers, and then factor by grouping.

Why Splitting the Middle Term Works: If \(m + n = b\), then \(mx + nx = (m + n)x = bx\). Splitting \(bx\) into \(mx + nx\) only rewrites the middle term; it does not change the value of the expression, but it makes grouping possible.

Real-World Application: The height of a thrown ball can be described by a quadratic expression. Factoring helps us find when the height is zero, which tells us when the ball reaches the ground.

AC Method for Factoring

To factor \(ax^{2} + bx + c\) where \(a \neq 1\):

  1. Multiply \(a \times c\), then find two numbers with product \(ac\) and sum \(b\). Use the sign rules from 3.2.
  2. Split the middle term \(bx\) using those two numbers.
  3. Factor by grouping, then check by expanding.
Example: Factoring by Grouping

Factor \(x^{3} + 3x^{2} + 2x + 6\).

Group the terms in pairs: \((x^{3} + 3x^{2}) + (2x + 6)\).

Factor each pair: \(x^{2}(x + 3) + 2(x + 3)\).

Both parts contain \(x + 3\), so the result is \((x + 3)(x^{2} + 2)\).

Check: \(x(x^{2} + 2) = x^{3} + 2x\) and \(3(x^{2} + 2) = 3x^{2} + 6\), which add back to the original.

Example: Full AC Method

Factor \(2x^{2} + 7x + 3\).

Here \(a = 2\), \(b = 7\), and \(c = 3\), so \(ac = 6\). We need numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 7: 6 and 1.

Split the middle term: \(2x^{2} + 6x + x + 3\).

Group: \((2x^{2} + 6x) + (x + 3) = 2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3)\).

Take the common factor \(x + 3\): \((x + 3)(2x + 1)\).

Check by expanding: \((x + 3)(2x + 1) = 2x^{2} + 7x + 3\).

Example: AC Method with a Negative Constant

Factor \(3x^{2} - 7x - 6\).

Here \(a = 3\), \(b = -7\), and \(c = -6\), so \(ac = -18\).

We need numbers with product \(-18\) and sum \(-7\). Since the product is negative, the signs are opposite.

Try \(-9\) and \(2\): \((-9)(2) = -18\) and \(-9 + 2 = -7\).

Split the middle term: \(3x^{2} - 9x + 2x - 6\).

Group: \((3x^{2} - 9x) + (2x - 6) = 3x(x - 3) + 2(x - 3)\).

Take the common factor \(x - 3\): \((x - 3)(3x + 2)\).

Check by expanding: \((x - 3)(3x + 2) = 3x^{2} + 2x - 9x - 6 = 3x^{2} - 7x - 6\).

3.4 Visualizing Factoring with an Area Model

Algebra tiles give a picture of factoring. A large square represents \(x^{2}\), a rectangle represents \(x\), and a small square represents 1. If all the tiles can be arranged into a rectangle, the side lengths of that rectangle are the factors.

Concrete Steps:

  1. Place the \(x^{2}\) tile in the upper-left corner. Its sides are \(x\) by \(x\), so it sets the starting width and height.
  2. Attach \(x\) tiles along the right side and along the bottom of the \(x^{2}\) tile. These make strips of width \(x\).
  3. Fill the remaining corner with unit tiles. That corner has area equal to the constant term.
  4. Read the total width and height along the outside edges. These two lengths are the factors.

Connection to Geometry: The area of a rectangle is length \(\times\) width. For \(x^{2} + 5x + 6\), one \(x^{2}\) tile, five \(x\) tiles, and six unit tiles form a rectangle.

The sides are \(x + 2\) and \(x + 3\), which matches the algebra: \((x + 2)(x + 3) = x^{2} + 5x + 6\).

Example: Building \(x^{2} + 7x + 12\)

We need one \(x^{2}\) tile, seven \(x\) tiles, and twelve unit tiles.

Put the \(x^{2}\) tile first. Split the seven \(x\) tiles into three along one side and four along the other, since \(3 + 4 = 7\).

The corner is then \(3 \times 4 = 12\) unit tiles, exactly the constant term.

The rectangle has sides \(x + 3\) and \(x + 4\), so \(x^{2} + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)\).

Check: \((x + 3)(x + 4) = x^{2} + 4x + 3x + 12 = x^{2} + 7x + 12\).

3.5 Powers and Products in Factoring

Powers are repeated multiplication, so \(x^{3} = x \times x \times x\). When factoring, take out the smallest power of each variable that appears in every term.

Key Idea: For powers of the same variable, the GCF is the smallest power appearing in every term. For \(x^{4}\), \(x^{2}\), and \(x^{3}\), the GCF is \(x^{2}\), since \(x^{2}\) divides all three and no higher power does.

Key Rules:

  • \(a^{m} \times a^{n} = a^{m+n}\), because \(m\) factors and \(n\) factors are combined.
  • \(a^{m} \div a^{n} = a^{m-n}\) for \(m > n\), because common factors cancel.
  • \((a^{m})^{n} = a^{mn}\), because there are \(n\) groups of \(m\) factors.
Example 3: Factoring with Powers

Factor \(2x^{3} + 4x^{2} - 6x\).

The coefficients 2, 4, and 6 all share 2, and every term contains at least one \(x\). The common factor is \(2x\).

Divide each term by \(2x\): \(2x(x^{2} + 2x - 3)\).

Factor the quadratic inside: \(2x(x + 3)(x - 1)\).

Check by expanding: \(2x(x^{2} + 2x - 3) = 2x^{3} + 4x^{2} - 6x\).

Why This Helps: In \(2x(x + 3)(x - 1) = 0\), the zero-product property gives \(x = 0\), \(x = -3\), or \(x = 1\).

3.6 Factoring with Rational Coefficients

When coefficients are fractions, factor out a common fraction, or multiply by the lowest common denominator (LCD) to work with whole numbers. For an expression, keep the common fraction outside; for an equation, multiply both sides by the LCD.

Why This Technique Works: Multiplying both sides of an equation by a nonzero number does not change its solutions, and it makes the arithmetic simpler. Always check by expanding or by substituting back into the original equation.

Example: Factoring Fractional Coefficients

Factor \(\frac{1}{2}x^{2} + \frac{3}{2}x + 1\).

Write \(1\) as \(\frac{2}{2}\) and take out the common factor \(\frac{1}{2}\): \(\frac{1}{2}(x^{2} + 3x + 2)\).

The trinomial factors as \((x + 1)(x + 2)\), so the result is \(\frac{1}{2}(x + 1)(x + 2)\).

Check: \(\frac{1}{2}(x^{2} + 3x + 2) = \frac{1}{2}x^{2} + \frac{3}{2}x + 1\).

Example: Clearing Denominators in an Equation

Solve \(\frac{1}{2}x^{2} + \frac{3}{2}x + 1 = 0\).

Multiply both sides by the LCD, 2: \(x^{2} + 3x + 2 = 0\).

Factor: \((x + 1)(x + 2) = 0\).

By the zero-product property, \(x + 1 = 0\) or \(x + 2 = 0\).

So \(x = -1\) or \(x = -2\). Both values check in the original equation.

3.7 Factoring Special Cases

Some expressions follow patterns that let you factor them quickly. Look for a difference of squares first, then check for a perfect square trinomial.

  • Perfect Square Trinomials: \(a^{2} + 2ab + b^{2} = (a + b)^{2}\). The middle term is twice the product of the two square roots.
  • Difference of Squares: \(a^{2} - b^{2} = (a - b)(a + b)\). There must be a subtraction sign.
  • Sum of Squares: \(a^{2} + b^{2}\) cannot be factored into simpler binomials using real numbers, so leave it as it is.
Example 4: Perfect Square Trinomial

Factor \(9x^{2} + 24x + 16\).

Notice \(9x^{2} = (3x)^{2}\), \(16 = 4^{2}\), and \(24x = 2(3x)(4)\).

So \(9x^{2} + 24x + 16 = (3x + 4)^{2}\).

Check: \((3x + 4)^{2} = 9x^{2} + 24x + 16\).

Motivation: This pattern helps when rewriting a quadratic in vertex form, which reveals the vertex of its graph.

Example 5: Difference of Squares

Factor \(x^{2} - 9\).

Write each term as a square: \(x^{2} = (x)^{2}\) and \(9 = 3^{2}\).

There is a subtraction sign, so use \(a^{2} - b^{2} = (a - b)(a + b)\) with \(a = x\) and \(b = 3\).

Therefore \(x^{2} - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)\).

Check: \((x - 3)(x + 3) = x^{2} + 3x - 3x - 9 = x^{2} - 9\).

Remember: A sum of squares such as \(x^{2} + 9\) does not factor into real binomials. The subtraction sign is essential.

3.8 Check Your Understanding

Try These
  1. Factor \(x^{2} + 9x + 20\).
  2. Factor \(4x^{2} - 25\).
  3. Solve \(x^{2} - 7x + 12 = 0\).
Answers with Brief Work

1. Numbers with product \(20\) and sum \(9\) are \(4\) and \(5\), so \(x^{2} + 9x + 20 = (x + 4)(x + 5)\).

2. Since \(4x^{2} = (2x)^{2}\) and \(25 = 5^{2}\), this is a difference of squares: \(4x^{2} - 25 = (2x - 5)(2x + 5)\).

3. Numbers with product \(12\) and sum \(-7\) are \(-3\) and \(-4\). Then \((x - 3)(x - 4) = 0\), so \(x = 3\) or \(x = 4\).

Bonus: A Difference of Cubes Pattern

A useful pattern is \(a^{3} - b^{3} = (a - b)(a^{2} + ab + b^{2})\). For example, \(x^{3} - 8 = (x - 2)(x^{2} + 2x + 4)\), since \(8 = 2^{3}\).

To check, expand: \(x(x^{2} + 2x + 4) = x^{3} + 2x^{2} + 4x\) and \(-2(x^{2} + 2x + 4) = -2x^{2} - 4x - 8\). The middle terms cancel to give \(x^{3} - 8\).
