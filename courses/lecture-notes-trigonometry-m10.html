Lecture 2: Trigonometry

Mathematics 10 - Chapter 2

2.1 The Tangent Ratio

In a right triangle, the tangent ratio relates the opposite side to the adjacent side. The tangent function is one of the three primary trigonometric ratios.

Why Trigonometry Matters: Trigonometry is the study of relationships between angles and sides in triangles. It's used in navigation, engineering, physics, computer graphics, and many other fields. The ancient Greeks used it to measure the Earth's circumference and distances to stars.

Tangent Ratio Definition

For angle \(\theta\) in a right triangle:

\(\tan \theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\)

Memory tip: "TOA" from SOH-CAH-TOA - Tangent is Opposite over Adjacent

Example 1: Finding Height of a Tree

A surveyor stands 20 meters from a tree and measures the angle to the top as 35°. How tall is the tree?

Using \(\tan 35° = \frac{\text{height}}{20}\), we get height = \(20 \times \tan 35° \approx 14\) meters

Motivation: This technique is used by surveyors, foresters, and arborists to measure heights of trees, buildings, and mountains without climbing them.

2.2 Using the Tangent Ratio to Calculate Lengths

To find a missing length using tangent, follow these steps:

  1. Draw and label the right triangle, identifying the given angle and known side
  2. Identify which side you need to find (opposite or adjacent)
  3. Use the tangent ratio to set up an equation: \(\tan \theta = \frac{\text{known}}{\text{unknown}}\)
  4. Solve for the unknown length

Real-World Applications: Trigonometry is used in construction to ensure structures are square, in navigation to find distances, and in sports to calculate trajectories.

2.3 Math Lab: Measuring an Inaccessible Height

Trigonometry allows us to measure heights and distances that are difficult to measure directly. The key is to create a right triangle where one dimension is accessible.

Procedure:

  1. Measure a distance from the object (baseline)
  2. Measure the angle of elevation to the top of the object
  3. Use the tangent ratio to calculate the height

Accuracy Considerations: The angle measurement is the biggest source of error. A small error in angle can lead to large errors in calculated height. Using a longer baseline improves accuracy.

2.4 The Sine and Cosine Ratios

The sine ratio relates the opposite side to the hypotenuse. The cosine ratio relates the adjacent side to the hypotenuse.

Connection to Real World: These ratios are essential for understanding waves, sound, light, and alternating current. The sine function describes the vertical displacement of a point on a spinning wheel.

SOH-CAH-TOA

\(\sin \theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\)
\(\cos \theta = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\)
\(\tan \theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}\)

Memory tip: SOH (sine is opposite over hypotenuse), CAH (cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse), TOA (tangent is opposite over adjacent)

2.5 Using the Sine and Cosine Ratios to Calculate Lengths

To find a missing length using sine or cosine:

  1. Draw and label the right triangle
  2. Identify the angle and the known side
  3. Choose the appropriate ratio (sine or cosine) based on which sides are involved
  4. Set up the equation and solve for the unknown
Example 2: Finding the Hypotenuse

A right triangle has an angle of 40° and the adjacent side is 8 cm. Find the hypotenuse.

Using \(\cos 40° = \frac{8}{\text{hypotenuse}}\), we get hypotenuse = \(\frac{8}{\cos 40°} \approx 10.9\) cm

Motivation: This is used in construction to find the length of rafters, and in navigation to find the direct distance between two points.

2.6 Applying the Trigonometric Ratios

Trigonometry is used to measure inaccessible heights such as buildings, trees, and mountains. It's also used in navigation, surveying, and engineering.

Common Applications:

  • Height of buildings or trees (using angle of elevation)
  • Distance across rivers or lakes (using triangulation)
  • Angles of elevation and depression in architecture
  • Navigation and bearings in aviation and maritime
  • Calculating forces in physics (horizontal and vertical components)

2.7 Solving Problems Involving More than One Right Triangle

Some problems require working with multiple right triangles. Break the problem into smaller triangles and apply trigonometric ratios to each one.

Example: A person observes two buildings. The angle to the top of the first building is 30°, and the angle to the top of the second building (behind the first) is 45°. Use the difference in angles to find the height of the second building.

Example 3: Two Building Problem

From 50 m away, the angle to the top of building A is 30° and to building B (taller) is 45°. What is the height difference?

Height of A: \(50 \times \tan 30° \approx 28.9\) m

Height of B: \(50 \times \tan 45° = 50\) m

Difference: \(50 - 28.9 = 21.1\) m

Motivation: This technique is used in surveying to find relative heights of structures.
