Lecture 2: Trigonometry

Mathematics 10 - Chapter 2

2.1 The Tangent Ratio

Trigonometry connects angles to side lengths. We start with a right triangle, a triangle with one \(90^\circ\) angle. The two sides that meet at that angle are the legs. The side across from the \(90^\circ\) angle is the hypotenuse, and it is always the longest side.

Side names depend on the angle you choose. An acute angle is an angle between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\). For an acute angle \(\theta\), the opposite side is the leg across from \(\theta\), and the adjacent side is the leg beside \(\theta\) that is not the hypotenuse. Choose a different angle and the opposite and adjacent labels switch.

Set your calculator to degree mode before using the trig keys, since the angles here are measured in degrees. A quick check is that \(\tan 45^\circ=1\); if your calculator shows something else, change its angle mode.

The ratios work because of similar triangles: if two right triangles share the same acute angle \(\theta\), they have the same shape, so \(\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\) has the same value in both, no matter how large the triangles are. That fixed value is called \(\tan \theta\).

Why Trigonometry Matters: Surveyors, architects, pilots, and game designers use these ratios to find heights and distances that cannot be measured directly.

Tangent Ratio Definition

For an acute angle \(\theta\) in a right triangle:

\(\tan \theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\)

Memory tip: "TOA" in SOH-CAH-TOA means Tangent = Opposite over Adjacent.

Example 1: Finding Height of a Tree

A surveyor stands \(20\text{ m}\) from a tree and measures an angle of elevation of \(35^\circ\) to the top. How tall is the tree?

  1. Choose the angle. Use the \(35^\circ\) angle at the surveyor.
  2. Label the sides. The height \(h\) is opposite \(35^\circ\) and the \(20\text{ m}\) baseline is adjacent. The ground and the tree form a right angle.
  3. Write the ratio. Opposite and adjacent call for tangent: \(\tan\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\), so \(\tan 35^\circ=\frac{h}{20}\).
  4. Substitute. In degree mode, \(\tan 35^\circ\approx 0.700\), so \(0.700\approx\frac{h}{20}\).
  5. Solve. Multiply both sides by \(20\): \(h=20\times \tan 35^\circ\approx 14\text{ m}\).
  6. Check reasonableness. Because \(\tan 35^\circ\) is less than \(1\), the tree should be shorter than the \(20\text{ m}\) baseline; \(14\text{ m}\) is.

Motivation: This lets foresters and arborists measure trees without climbing them.

2.2 Using the Tangent Ratio to Calculate Lengths

Use this routine whenever a problem gives you an angle and one side of a right triangle. The words opposite and adjacent always refer to the angle you are using, not to the triangle as a whole. The hypotenuse is always the longest side, across from the \(90^\circ\) angle.

  1. Draw the triangle and mark the given angle \(\theta\)
  2. Label the hypotenuse, then label the opposite and adjacent legs relative to \(\theta\)
  3. Pick the ratio that uses the side you know and the side you want: \(\tan \theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\)
  4. Substitute the numbers, solve for the unknown, and include the units

Example check: If the adjacent leg is \(12\text{ m}\) and \(\theta=25^\circ\), then \(\tan 25^\circ=\frac{x}{12}\), so \(x=12\times \tan 25^\circ\approx 5.6\text{ m}\).

If the opposite leg is known instead, the unknown adjacent leg ends up in the denominator. From \(\tan\theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\), multiply by the adjacent leg and then divide: \(\text{adj}=\frac{\text{opp}}{\tan\theta}\).

Worked Example: Finding the Run of a Ramp

A ramp rises \(2\text{ m}\) at an angle of \(15^\circ\) to the ground. How far does the ramp run along the ground?

  1. Choose the angle. Use the \(15^\circ\) angle where the ramp meets the ground.
  2. Label the sides. The \(2\text{ m}\) rise is opposite the \(15^\circ\) angle and the horizontal run \(r\) is adjacent.
  3. Write the ratio. \(\tan 15^\circ=\frac{2}{r}\).
  4. Solve. Multiply by \(r\), then divide by \(\tan 15^\circ\): \(r=\frac{2}{\tan 15^\circ}\approx 7.5\text{ m}\).
  5. Check reasonableness. A \(15^\circ\) ramp is shallow, so the run should be much longer than the \(2\text{ m}\) rise; \(7.5\text{ m}\) makes sense.

Key idea: When the wanted side is adjacent, divide the known opposite side by the tangent of the angle.

Real-World Applications: Trigonometry is used in construction to check that structures are square, in navigation to find distances, and in sports to study trajectories.

2.3 Angles of Elevation and Depression

When you look up from a horizontal line, the angle you turn through is the angle of elevation. When you look down, it is the angle of depression. Both are measured from a horizontal line, not from the vertical or from the sloping ground.

To measure an inaccessible height, make a right triangle with one side that you can actually measure. The angle is measured from eye level, so any rise you calculate also starts at eye level.

Procedure:

  1. Measure a horizontal distance from the object (the baseline)
  2. Measure the angle of elevation from eye level to the top of the object
  3. Calculate the rise with \(\text{rise}=d\tan \theta\), where \(d\) is the baseline
  4. Add your eye height if the height you want is measured from the ground
Worked Example: Height of a Flagpole

An observer stands \(12\text{ m}\) from a flagpole and measures an angle of elevation of \(40^\circ\) to the top. The observer's eye is \(1.6\text{ m}\) above the ground. How tall is the flagpole?

  1. Choose the angle and label sides. Relative to \(40^\circ\), the rise from eye level to the top is opposite and the \(12\text{ m}\) baseline is adjacent.
  2. Write the ratio. \(\tan 40^\circ=\frac{\text{rise}}{12}\).
  3. Solve for the rise. \(\text{rise}=12\tan 40^\circ\approx 10.1\text{ m}\).
  4. Add the eye height. \(\text{height}\approx 10.1+1.6=11.7\text{ m}\).
  5. Check reasonableness. Since \(\tan 40^\circ\) is less than \(1\), the rise is less than the \(12\text{ m}\) baseline, and the total is close to, but a little less than, \(12\text{ m}\).

Why add eye height? The \(10.1\text{ m}\) rise starts at the eye, not at the ground. The pole extends from the ground to the top, so we add the \(1.6\text{ m}\) between the ground and the eye.

An angle of depression uses the same idea. Looking down from a height, the angle is measured from a horizontal line at the eye. The vertical drop from eye level and the horizontal distance form the same kind of right triangle, so the drop is \(d\tan\theta\) with that same angle. If the drop ends at ground or water level and you want the height of the place you are standing, subtract your eye height from that drop.

Accuracy Considerations: The angle measurement is the biggest source of error. A small error in the angle can lead to a large error in the calculated height, so measure carefully and use a longer baseline when you can.

2.4 The Sine and Cosine Ratios

Tangent is not enough when the hypotenuse is involved. The same similar-triangle idea gives two more fixed ratios for an angle \(\theta\): sine compares the opposite leg with the hypotenuse, and cosine compares the adjacent leg with the hypotenuse. Because all right triangles with the same \(\theta\) are similar, these ratios do not change with the size of the triangle.

SOH-CAH-TOA applies only to right triangles. It also depends on which angle you pick: opposite and adjacent are labels for that angle, so changing the angle changes the labels.

Connection to Real World: Sine and cosine also describe waves and rotating motion, such as the vertical position of a point on a spinning wheel.

SOH-CAH-TOA

In a right triangle, for the acute angle \(\theta\):

\(\sin \theta=\frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\)

\(\cos \theta=\frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\)

\(\tan \theta=\frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}\)

Memory tip: SOH, CAH, TOA gives the order of the words in each fraction.

Remember: These ratios need a \(90^\circ\) angle, and opposite and adjacent are always named after the chosen angle.

2.5 Using the Sine and Cosine Ratios to Calculate Lengths

Use the same four-step routine as before. The new decision is choosing between sine and cosine: look at the leg you know or want and ask whether it is opposite or adjacent to the given angle. The hypotenuse is the side across from the \(90^\circ\) angle.

  1. Draw the right triangle and label the hypotenuse, opposite, and adjacent sides relative to the given angle
  2. Choose the ratio that links the known side and the unknown side
  3. Substitute the angle and the known length into the ratio
  4. Solve for the unknown and round to a sensible precision
Example 2: Finding the Hypotenuse

A right triangle has an angle of \(40^\circ\) and an adjacent leg of \(8\text{ cm}\). Find the hypotenuse.

The adjacent leg and the hypotenuse point to cosine: \(\cos 40^\circ=\frac{8}{h}\).

\(h=\frac{8}{\cos 40^\circ}\approx 10.4\text{ cm}\)

Check reasonableness: The hypotenuse must be the longest side, and \(10.4\text{ cm}\) is greater than \(8\text{ cm}\).

Motivation: This finds rafter lengths in construction and direct distances in navigation.

Worked Example: Finding an Opposite Leg

A right triangle has a hypotenuse of \(10\text{ cm}\) and an angle of \(30^\circ\). Find the leg opposite the \(30^\circ\) angle.

  1. Choose the angle and label sides. The known side is the hypotenuse and the wanted side is opposite \(30^\circ\).
  2. Write the ratio. Opposite and hypotenuse call for sine: \(\sin 30^\circ=\frac{x}{10}\).
  3. Solve. Multiply by \(10\): \(x=10\sin 30^\circ=5\text{ cm}\).
  4. Check reasonableness. The opposite leg must be shorter than the hypotenuse, and \(5\text{ cm}\) is half of \(10\text{ cm}\).

Key idea: When the wanted side is on top of the fraction, multiply the known side by the ratio.

2.6 Applying the Trigonometric Ratios

In an application, first decide which angle and which sides the problem gives you. Then use SOH-CAH-TOA to turn the geometry into one short equation. Angles of elevation and depression are measured from horizontal lines, so a right triangle is usually hiding in the diagram.

Known / Wanted / Ratio Decision Guide:

Known / Wanted / Ratio

Known angle and adjacent; want opposite \(\to\) tangent, then multiply: \(\tan\theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\).

Known angle and opposite; want adjacent \(\to\) tangent, then divide: \(\text{adj}=\frac{\text{opp}}{\tan\theta}\).

Known angle and hypotenuse; want opposite \(\to\) sine: \(\sin\theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}\).

Known angle and hypotenuse; want adjacent \(\to\) cosine: \(\cos\theta=\frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}\).

Known angle and leg; want hypotenuse \(\to\) sine if the leg is opposite, cosine if it is adjacent.

Common Applications:

  • Height of a building or tree from a measured baseline (angle of elevation)
  • Distance across a river using a baseline and an angle from each end (triangulation)
  • Drop from a cliff or aircraft (angle of depression)
  • Navigation using bearings measured clockwise from north
  • Splitting a force into horizontal and vertical components in physics

Common Mistakes:

  • Using SOH-CAH-TOA in a triangle with no \(90^\circ\) angle
  • Calling a side opposite or adjacent before choosing which angle to use
  • Treating the hypotenuse as the adjacent leg (the hypotenuse is never adjacent)
  • Forgetting degree mode before using a trig key on the calculator
  • Dividing by the wrong side when the adjacent leg is unknown: from \(\tan\theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\), \(\text{adj}=\frac{\text{opp}}{\tan\theta}\)
  • Forgetting to account for eye height when the height is measured from the ground

2.7 Solving Problems Involving More than One Right Triangle

When a diagram has two right triangles, solve them one at a time instead of trying to combine the angles. If both triangles share the same horizontal baseline and the same observation point, compute each height separately and then subtract. The difference between the two angles is not itself an angle in either triangle, so do not use it in a ratio.

Example: An observer looks at the top of a shorter building at an angle of elevation of \(30^\circ\) and at the top of a taller building behind it at \(45^\circ\). Use the same baseline for both calculations, find each height, and then subtract to get the height difference.

Example 3: Two-Building Problem

From \(50\text{ m}\) away, the angle of elevation to building A is \(30^\circ\) and to building B is \(45^\circ\). Both angles use the same baseline.

  1. Set up triangle A. The \(50\text{ m}\) baseline is adjacent and the height \(h_A\) is opposite \(30^\circ\), so \(\tan 30^\circ=\frac{h_A}{50}\).
  2. Solve for \(h_A\). \(h_A=50\tan 30^\circ\approx 28.9\text{ m}\).
  3. Set up triangle B. With the same baseline and \(45^\circ\), \(\tan 45^\circ=\frac{h_B}{50}\).
  4. Solve for \(h_B\). \(h_B=50\tan 45^\circ=50\text{ m}\).
  5. Subtract the heights. \(50-28.9=21.1\text{ m}\).
  6. Check reasonableness. A \(45^\circ\) sightline gives a height equal to the baseline, and \(30^\circ\) gives a smaller height, so B should be taller by less than \(50\text{ m}\); \(21.1\text{ m}\) fits.

Note: Never use \(45^\circ-30^\circ=15^\circ\) in a ratio; that difference is not an angle in either right triangle.

Motivation: Surveyors use this to compare heights without reaching either roof.

2.8 Check Your Understanding

Work on a separate page first, then compare your answers below.

  1. An acute angle of \(20^\circ\) has an opposite leg of \(5\text{ m}\). Find the adjacent leg.
  2. A right triangle has a hypotenuse of \(10\text{ cm}\) and an angle of \(30^\circ\). Find the opposite leg.
  3. A person with eye height \(1.5\text{ m}\) stands \(8\text{ m}\) from a tree and measures an angle of elevation of \(50^\circ\) to the top. Find the tree's height.

Answers:

  1. \(\text{adjacent}=\frac{5}{\tan 20^\circ}\approx 13.7\text{ m}\)
  2. \(\text{opposite}=10\sin 30^\circ=5\text{ cm}\)
  3. \(\text{height}=8\tan 50^\circ+1.5\approx 9.5+1.5\approx 11.0\text{ m}\)

Bonus: Why Elevation and Depression Angles Match

Imagine two people at different heights looking at each other. The horizontal line through each person's eye is parallel to the other, and the line of sight crosses both like a transversal. The angle one person looks down (depression) equals the angle the other looks up (elevation) because they are alternate interior angles.

That is why a surveyor can measure an angle of depression from a hilltop and still use \(\tan \theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\) with the same angle in the triangle below.
