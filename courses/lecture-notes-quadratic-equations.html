Quadratic Equations

Precalculus 11 - Lecture Notes
British Columbia Curriculum | Last Updated: 2026

1. Introduction to Quadratic Equations

A quadratic equation is an equation of the form:

\[ax^2 + bx + c = 0\]

Where \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are constants, and \(a \neq 0\). When \(a = 0\), the equation is linear, not quadratic.

Definition: A quadratic equation has at most two solutions, called roots or x-intercepts. These solutions can be real or complex numbers. The solutions are the x-coordinates where the parabola \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\) crosses the x-axis.

1.1 Standard vs. Quadratic Forms

All quadratic equations can be written in standard form, but some have special forms that suggest specific solving strategies:

  • Standard form: \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\)
  • Empty quadratic: \(ax^2 + c = 0\) (no x-term), solve with square root method
  • Perfect square trinomial: \((x + p)^2 = 0\), one repeated solution

2. The Square Root Method

The square root method is the simplest technique for solving quadratics of the form \(x^2 = c\) or \(ax^2 = c\).

Square Root Property: If \(x^2 = c\), then \(x = \sqrt{c}\) or \(x = -\sqrt{c}\) (when \(c \geq 0\)). If \(c < 0\), the solutions are complex: \(x = \pm i\sqrt{|c|}\).

2.1 Solving \(x^2 = c\)

Example 1: Positive Constant

Solve \(x^2 = 25\).

Solution:

\[x = \pm \sqrt{25} = \pm 5\]

Solutions: \(x = 5\) or \(x = -5\)

Check: \(5^2 = 25\) and \((-5)^2 = 25\) ✓

Example 2: Negative Constant

Solve \(x^2 = -9\).

Solution:

Since we cannot take the square root of a negative number in the real numbers, there are no real solutions.

In the complex numbers: \(x = \pm 3i\)

2.2 Solving \(ax^2 = c\)

Example 3: Isolating x²

Solve \(3x^2 = 27\).

Solution:

Divide both sides by 3: \(x^2 = 9\)

Take square root: \(x = \pm 3\)

Solutions: \(x = 3\) or \(x = -3\)

2.3 Clearing Fractions and Radicals

Example 4: Fractional Coefficient

Solve \(\frac{2x^2}{3} = 8\).

Solution:

Multiply both sides by \(\frac{3}{2}\): \(x^2 = 12\)

\[x = \pm \sqrt{12} = \pm \sqrt{4 \cdot 3} = \pm 2\sqrt{3}\]

3. Graphical Methods

The graph of \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\) is a parabola. The solutions to \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) are the x-intercepts (where the parabola crosses the x-axis).

3.1 Reading Solutions from a Graph

Example 5: Graphical Solution

Solve \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\) graphically.

Solution:

Graph \(y = x^2 - 5x + 6\)

The parabola opens upward (a > 0) and crosses the x-axis at \(x = 2\) and \(x = 3\)

Solutions: \(x = 2\) or \(x = 3\)

3.2 How Many Solutions?

The discriminant tells us about the graph:

D Value Graph Shows Solutions
D > 0 Parabola crosses x-axis at 2 points 2 distinct real solutions
D = 0 Parabola touches x-axis at 1 point (vertex) 1 repeated solution
D < 0 Parabola does not cross x-axis 0 real solutions (2 complex)

3.3 Using Graphs to Estimate

Graphs can help estimate solutions and check algebra:

Connection:

The x-intercepts of the graph correspond to the solutions found algebraically. All three methods give the same roots!

4. Solving by Factoring

Factoring is one of the most efficient methods when the quadratic expression can be written as a product of simpler factors.

Zero Product Property:
If \((x - r_1)(x - r_2) = 0\), then \(x = r_1\) or \(x = r_2\)

3.1 Connection to x-Intercepts

When we factor \(ax^2 + bx + c = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2) = 0\), the solutions \(x = r_1\) and \(x = r_2\) correspond exactly to the x-intercepts of the parabola. This is why the factored form is so useful!

3.2 Factoring Methods

  • Common factoring: Factor out GCD first
  • Difference of squares: \(a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)\)
  • Trinomial factoring: \(ax^2 + bx + c = (dx + e)(fx + g)\)
Example 5: Factoring a Trinomial

Solve \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\) by factoring.

Solution:

Find two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to -5: -2 and -3

\[x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0\]

By the Zero Product Property: \(x - 2 = 0\) or \(x - 3 = 0\)

Solutions: \(x = 2\) or \(x = 3\)

3.3 Factoring with \(a \neq 1\)

Example 6: AC Method

Solve \(2x^2 + 7x + 3 = 0\) by factoring.

Solution:

Multiply \(a \times c = 2 \times 3 = 6\)

Find two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 7: 6 and 1

Rewrite: \(2x^2 + 6x + x + 3 = 0\)

Factor by grouping: \(2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3) = 0\)

Factor out common: \((2x + 1)(x + 3) = 0\)

Solutions: \(x = -\frac{1}{2}\) or \(x = -3\)

3.4 Difference of Squares

Example 7: Special Pattern

Solve \(x^2 - 16 = 0\).

Solution:

This is a difference of squares: \(x^2 - 4^2 = (x + 4)(x - 4) = 0\)

Solutions: \(x = -4\) or \(x = 4\)

4. Completing the Square

Completing the square transforms a quadratic into vertex form, revealing both the vertex and the solutions.

4.1 The Method

Example 8: Step-by-Step

Solve \(x^2 + 6x - 7 = 0\) by completing the square.

Solution:

Step 1: Move constant: \(x^2 + 6x = 7\)

Step 2: Complete the square: Add \((\frac{b}{2})^2 = (\frac{6}{2})^2 = 9\) to both sides

\[x^2 + 6x + 9 = 7 + 9\]

Step 3: Factor: \((x + 3)^2 = 16\)

Step 4: Take square root: \(x + 3 = \pm 4\)

Step 5: Solve: \(x = -3 \pm 4\)

Solutions: \(x = 1\) or \(x = -7\)

4.2 When \(a \neq 1\)

Example 9: Leading Coefficient

Solve \(3x^2 + 12x + 9 = 0\) by completing the square.

Solution:

Step 1: Divide by 3: \(x^2 + 4x + 3 = 0\)

Step 2: Move constant: \(x^2 + 4x = -3\)

Step 3: Complete: \((\frac{4}{2})^2 = 4\)

\[x^2 + 4x + 4 = -3 + 4\]

\[(x + 2)^2 = 1\]

Step 4: \(x + 2 = \pm 1\)

Solutions: \(x = -1\) or \(x = -3\)

4.3 Vertex Form Connection

Completing the square transforms \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) into \(a(x - h)^2 + k = 0\), where \((h, k)\) is the vertex. The solutions appear when we solve for \(x\): \(x = h \pm \sqrt{-k/a}\).

5. The Quadratic Formula

The quadratic formula is derived from completing the square on the general form \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\). It works for ALL quadratic equations.

\[x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\]

5.1 Derivation (Completing the Square)

Starting with \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) (assuming \(a \neq 0\)):

  1. Divide by \(a\): \(x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{c}{a} = 0\)
  2. Move constant: \(x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}\)
  3. Complete square: Add \((\frac{b}{2a})^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2}\)
  4. \[\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}\]
  5. Take square root: \(x + \frac{b}{2a} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\)
  6. Solve: \(x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\)

This shows the quadratic formula is just completing the square with letters instead of numbers!

5.2 The Discriminant

The Discriminant:
\[D = b^2 - 4ac\]

The discriminant tells us the nature of the solutions:

D Value Number of Solutions Solution Type
\(D > 0\) 2 distinct solutions Real and different
\(D = 0\) 1 solution (repeated) Real and equal
\(D < 0\) 0 real solutions Two complex conjugates

5.3 Using the Formula

Example 10: Applying the Formula

Solve \(2x^2 + 3x - 2 = 0\).

Solution:

\(a = 2\), \(b = 3\), \(c = -2\)

\[x = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{9 - 4(2)(-2)}}{2(2)} = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{25}}{4}\]

\[x = \frac{-3 \pm 5}{4}\]

Solutions: \(x = \frac{1}{2}\) or \(x = -2\)

Common Error Alert:

Always write DOWN the values of \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) before substituting into the formula. It's easy to misread coefficients!

6. Choosing the Best Method

Understanding when to use each method saves time and reduces errors:

Form of Equation Best Method Why?
\(x^2 = c\) (no x-term) Square Root Method Direct and fastest
Factorable with integers Factoring Quickest for simple numbers
\(a = 1\), even \(b\) Completing the Square Reveals vertex and exact form
Any quadratic, especially with decimals/fractions Quadratic Formula Works every time
Real-world problems Any method + verification Check answers make sense
All Methods Are Connected:

Factoring ↔ Zeros of the polynomial ↔ x-intercepts of the graph. Completing the square and the quadratic formula are algebraically equivalent. The square root method is a special case of both when \(b = 0\).

7. Applications

7.1 Projectile Motion

The height of a projectile follows a quadratic path:

\[h(t) = -16t^2 + v_0 t + h_0\]

where \(v_0\) = initial velocity (ft/s) and \(h_0\) = initial height (ft)

Example 11: Ball Trajectory

A ball is thrown upward with velocity 64 ft/s from a height of 6 ft. When does it hit the ground?

Solution:

\[h(t) = -16t^2 + 64t + 6 = 0\]

Using quadratic formula: \(t = \frac{-64 \pm \sqrt{4096 + 384}}{-32}\)

\[t = \frac{-64 \pm 67.2}{-32}\]

Positive solution: \(t \approx 4.1\) seconds

7.2 Area and Geometry Problems

Example 12: Rectangle Dimensions

A rectangular garden has area 60 m². The length is 4 m more than the width. Find the dimensions.

Solution:

Let \(w\) = width. Then length = \(w + 4\)

Area equation: \(w(w + 4) = 60\)

Expand: \(w^2 + 4w - 60 = 0\)

Factoring: \((w + 10)(w - 6) = 0\)

\(w = 6\) (width must be positive)

Dimensions: Width = 6 m, Length = 10 m

7.3 Optimization: Maximum/Minimum

Setting the derivative of a quadratic to zero gives the vertex, which is the optimal value.
