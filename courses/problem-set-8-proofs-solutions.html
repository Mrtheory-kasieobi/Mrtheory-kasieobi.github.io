Solutions: Problem Set 8

Introduction to Proofs - Lecture 8

Key ideas used in these solutions

  • Strong induction allows the hypothesis for all earlier indices, not just the immediately preceding one.
  • Well-ordering says every nonempty set of positive integers has a least element.
  • Existence of prime factorisations splits \(n\) into smaller factors and applies the hypothesis to each.
  • The number of base cases should match how far back the recurrence looks.

Worked solutions

Problem 1: Prove by strong induction that every integer \(n\ge2\) is a sum of copies of \(2\) and \(3\).
Base cases. \(2=2\) and \(3=3\).
Step. Let \(n\ge4\) and assume every integer \(m\) with \(2\le m<n\) has such a representation. Then \(n-2\ge2\), so by the hypothesis \(n-2=2a+3b\) for some nonnegative integers \(a,b\). Hence \(n=2(a+1)+3b\).

Two base cases are not strictly necessary here (one could handle \(n=4=2+2\) and reduce by \(2\)), but \(2\) and \(3\) make the step uniform. This proof uses the hypothesis for \(n-2\), which ordinary induction would not provide.

Problem 2: Prove by strong induction that every integer \(n\ge2\) has a prime divisor.
Base case. \(2\) is prime and divides itself.
Step. Let \(n>2\), and assume every integer \(m\) with \(2\le m<n\) has a prime divisor. If \(n\) is prime, it is its own prime divisor. If \(n\) is composite, write \(n=ab\) with \(1<a<n\). By the hypothesis, \(a\) has a prime divisor \(p\). Then \(p\mid a\mid n\), so \(p\mid n\).

By strong induction every \(n\ge2\) has a prime divisor.

Problem 3: Determine whether the Fibonacci numbers satisfy \(a_n\ge n\) for all \(n\ge1\); if not, give a correct linear lower bound and prove it.
Base cases. \(a_1=1\ge1\) and \(a_2=1\ge2\) is false, so use \(a_2=1\) and start at \(n=3\): \(a_3=2\ge3\) is also false. The claim is false as stated (\(a_4=3<4\)).

The correct elementary bound is \(a_n\ge n\) for \(n\ge5\), or \(a_n\ge n/2\) for \(n\ge1\). To see \(a_n\ge n/2\): base cases \(a_1=1\ge1/2\), \(a_2=1\ge1\); if \(a_{n+1}\ge(n+1)/2\) and \(a_n\ge n/2\), then \(a_{n+2}\ge(2n+1)/2\ge(n+2)/2\) for \(n\ge1\). This illustrates that a plausible induction claim must be tested against the early terms.

Problem 4: Prove by strong induction that for \(n\ge1\), \(n\) can be written uniquely as \(2^a\cdot q\) with \(q\) odd.
Existence, strong induction. For \(n=1\), take \(a=0,q=1\). For \(n>1\), if \(n\) is odd take \(a=0,q=n\). If \(n\) is even, write \(n=2m\) with \(1\le m<n\); by the hypothesis \(m=2^a q\) with \(q\) odd, so \(n=2^{a+1}q\).
Uniqueness. Suppose \(2^a q=2^b r\) with \(q,r\) odd and \(a\le b\). Then \(q=2^{b-a}r\). If \(b>a\), the right side is even while \(q\) is odd, impossible. Hence \(a=b\) and \(q=r\).

This decomposition is the foundation of the 2-adic valuation; strong induction handles existence while a parity argument handles uniqueness.

Problem 5: Use well-ordering to prove: if \(S\subseteq\mathbb{N}\) and \(n\in S\) implies \(n+1\in S\), and \(1\in S\), then \(S=\mathbb{N}\).

This is precisely the induction principle derived in Theorem 8.3. Suppose \(S\ne\mathbb{N}\), and let \(m\) be the least positive integer not in \(S\) (well-ordering). Since \(1\in S\), \(m>1\). Then \(m-1\in S\), so by the closure hypothesis \(m=(m-1)+1\in S\), contradicting the choice of \(m\). Hence \(S=\mathbb{N}\).

Problem 6: Define \(b_0=0,b_1=1,b_2=2\), \(b_{n+3}=b_{n+2}+b_{n+1}+b_n\). Prove \(b_n\le3^n\) for all \(n\ge0\).
Base cases. \(0\le1\), \(1\le3\), \(2\le9\).
Step. Assume \(b_j\le3^j\) for \(j\le n+2\). Then \[b_{n+3}=b_{n+2}+b_{n+1}+b_n\le3^{n+2}+3^{n+1}+3^n=3^n(9+3+1)=13\cdot3^n\le27\cdot3^n=3^{n+3}.\] Since \(13\le27\), the bound holds.

Three base cases match the three previous terms used by the recurrence.

Problem 7: Explain the flaw: "For strong induction on \(P(n)\), assume \(P(k)\) for all \(k<n\); since \(P(n)\) follows from \(P(n-1)\), no base case is needed."

At \(n=1\) there is no \(k<1\) in the range of the statement, so the hypothesis is empty; the implication "empty hypothesis \(\to P(1)\)" would require proving \(P(1)\) directly. Moreover, claiming that \(P(n)\) follows from \(P(n-1)\) is precisely the inductive step and must be proved, not assumed. Without a base case, a chain of implications has no starting point: for example, the false statement "\(n=0\) for all \(n\)" satisfies \(P(k)\to P(k+1)\) only vacuously when \(P(k)\) is false, but fails at the base.

Problem 8: Prove by strong induction that \(3^n\) can be written as a sum of \(n\) odd positive integers for every \(n\ge1\).
Base cases. \(3=3\) is one odd positive integer, and \(9=1+7\) is a sum of two odd positive integers.
Step. Let \(k\ge2\) and assume \(3^k=o_1+\cdots+o_k\) with every \(o_i\) odd and positive. Then \[3^{k+1}=3\cdot3^k=3o_1+o_2+\cdots+o_k.\] The number \(3o_1\) is odd and positive, and together with the remaining \(k-1\) terms it gives a sum of exactly \(k+1\) odd positive integers.

Strong induction from the two base cases proves the claim for every \(n\ge1\). The separate treatment of \(k=1\) is necessary because \(3^{2}=9\) cannot be written as \(3o_1\) using a single term.

Bonus Problem: Prove by strong induction that \(2^n>n^2\) for every integer \(n\ge5\).
Base case: \(2^5=32>25=5^2\). Inductive step: assume \(2^k>k^2\) for all \(5\le k\le n\), where \(n\ge5\). Then \(2^{n+1}=2\cdot2^n>2n^2\). Since \(n\ge3\), \(2n^2-(n+1)^2=n^2-2n-1>0\), so \(2n^2>(n+1)^2\). Therefore \(2^{n+1}>(n+1)^2\).
