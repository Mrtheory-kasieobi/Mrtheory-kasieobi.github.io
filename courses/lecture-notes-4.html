Lecture 4: Congruences and Modular Arithmetic

Lecture 4Congruences and Modular Arithmetic

1. Definition of Congruence

Definition 1.1 (Congruence Modulo n) Let \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(n \in \mathbb{Z}^+\). We say that \(a\) is congruent to \(b\) modulo \(n\), written: \[a \equiv b \pmod{n}\] if \(n \mid (a - b)\). This is read as "\(a\) is congruent to \(b\) mod \(n\)".
Example 4.1 Verify the following congruences:
  • \(17 \equiv 5 \pmod{12}\) because \(17 - 5 = 12\) and \(12 \mid 12\)
  • \(7 \equiv -5 \pmod{12}\) because \(7 - (-5) = 12\)
  • \(20 \equiv 2 \pmod{6}\) because \(20 - 2 = 18\) and \(6 \mid 18\)
  • \(-3 \equiv 9 \pmod{12}\) because \(-3 - 9 = -12\) and \(12 \mid -12\)
Definition 1.2 (Residue Class) The set of all integers congruent to \(a\) modulo \(n\) is called the residue class of \(a\) modulo \(n\)**: \[[a]_n = \{x \in \mathbb{Z} : x \equiv a \pmod{n}\} = \{a + nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}\]
Definition 1.3 (Complete Residue System) A set of integers \(\{r_0, r_1, \ldots, r_{n-1}\}\) is called a complete residue system modulo \(n\)** if every integer is congruent to exactly one element of the set. The canonical complete residue system is \(\{0, 1, 2, \ldots, n-1\}\).
Example 4.2 Find the canonical residue class of 17 modulo 5.
Step 1: Divide 17 by 5

\(17 = 5 \cdot 3 + 2\)

Step 2: Identify the remainder

The remainder is 2, so \(17 \equiv 2 \pmod{5}\).

The canonical residue class is \([2]_5 = \{\ldots, -8, -3, 2, 7, 12, 17, \ldots\}\).

2. Properties of Congruences

Theorem 2.1 (Properties of Congruence) Let \(a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(n \in \mathbb{Z}^+\).
  1. Reflexivity: \(a \equiv a \pmod{n}\)
  2. Symmetry: If \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\), then \(b \equiv a \pmod{n}\)
  3. Transitivity: If \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\) and \(b \equiv c \pmod{n}\), then \(a \equiv c \pmod{n}\)
  4. Addition: If \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\) and \(c \equiv d \pmod{n}\), then \(a + c \equiv b + d \pmod{n}\)
  5. Multiplication: If \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\) and \(c \equiv d \pmod{n}\), then \(ac \equiv bd \pmod{n}\)
  6. Exponentiation: If \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\), then \(a^k \equiv b^k \pmod{n}\) for all \(k \geq 1\)
Proof of Theorem 2.1(6) - Exponentiation:
Step 1: Base case

For \(k = 1\): \(a^1 \equiv b^1 \pmod{n}\) is just \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\), which is true.

Step 2: Inductive step

Assume \(a^k \equiv b^k \pmod{n}\) for some \(k \geq 1\).

We need to show \(a^{k+1} \equiv b^{k+1} \pmod{n}\).

Step 3: Use multiplication property

We know:

  • \(a^k \equiv b^k \pmod{n}\) (inductive hypothesis)
  • \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\) (given)

By the multiplication property: \(a^k \cdot a \equiv b^k \cdot b \pmod{n}\)

Therefore \(a^{k+1} \equiv b^{k+1} \pmod{n}\).

Conclusion:

By mathematical induction, \(a^k \equiv b^k \pmod{n}\) for all \(k \geq 1\). \(\square\)

3. Solving Linear Congruences

Definition 3.1 (Linear Congruence) A congruence of the form: \[ax \equiv b \pmod{n}\] where \(a, b, n \in \mathbb{Z}\) with \(n > 0\), is called a linear congruence in one variable.
Theorem 3.1 (Existence and Uniqueness of Solutions) Let \(a, b, n \in \mathbb{Z}\) with \(n > 0\). The linear congruence \(ax \equiv b \pmod{n}\):
  1. Has a solution if and only if \(\gcd(a, n) \mid b\)
  2. If a solution exists, there are exactly \(\gcd(a, n)\) distinct solutions modulo \(n\)
Proof of Theorem 3.1(1):
(⇒) If a solution exists, then gcd(a,n) | b:

Suppose \(x_0\) is a solution, so \(ax_0 \equiv b \pmod{n}\).

This means \(n \mid (ax_0 - b)\), so \(ax_0 - b = nk\) for some integer \(k\).

Therefore \(ax_0 - nk = b\).

Since \(\gcd(a, n)\) divides both \(a\) and \(n\), it divides the left side: \(\gcd(a, n) \mid b\).

(⇐) If gcd(a,n) | b, then a solution exists:

Suppose \(d = \gcd(a, n)\) and \(d \mid b\).

Write \(a = da'\), \(n = dn'\), \(b = db'\) where \(a', n', b' \in \mathbb{Z}\).

Note: \(\gcd(a', n') = 1\) since we divided out the gcd.

The congruence becomes: \(da'x \equiv db' \pmod{dn'}\)

This means \(dn' \mid (da'x - db') = d(a'x - b')\).

Since \(d \neq 0\), this is equivalent to \(n' \mid (a'x - b')\).

So \(a'x \equiv b' \pmod{n'}\).

Since \(\gcd(a', n') = 1\), there exists \(x_0\) such that \(a'x_0 \equiv 1 \pmod{n'}\).

Then \(x = x_0 b'\) satisfies \(a'x \equiv b' \pmod{n'}\).

Therefore \(ax \equiv b \pmod{n}\) has a solution. \(\square\)

Example 3.1 Solve \(3x \equiv 4 \pmod{7}\).
Step 1: Check if solution exists

\(\gcd(3, 7) = 1\)

Since \(1 \mid 4\), a solution exists.

Since \(\gcd(3, 7) = 1\), the solution is unique modulo 7.

Step 2: Find the inverse of 3 modulo 7

We need \(x\) such that \(3x \equiv 1 \pmod{7}\).

Testing: \(3 \cdot 5 = 15 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}\)

So \(3^{-1} \equiv 5 \pmod{7}\).

Step 3: Multiply both sides by the inverse

\(x \equiv 5 \cdot 4 \equiv 20 \equiv 6 \pmod{7}\)

Check: \(3 \cdot 6 = 18 \equiv 4 \pmod{7}\). ✓

Example 3.2 Solve \(6x \equiv 9 \pmod{15}\).
Step 1: Check gcd

\(\gcd(6, 15) = 3\)

Since \(3 \mid 9\), solutions exist.

There will be 3 distinct solutions modulo 15.

Step 2: Divide through by gcd

Divide the congruence by 3: \(2x \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\)

Step 3: Solve the reduced congruence

Find the inverse of 2 modulo 5.

\(2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}\), so \(2^{-1} \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\).

\(x \equiv 3 \cdot 3 \equiv 9 \equiv 4 \pmod{5}\)

Step 4: Find all solutions modulo 15

The solutions are \(x \equiv 4 \pmod{5}\).

Modulo 15, the solutions are: \(x \equiv 4, 9, 14 \pmod{15}\)

Check: \(6 \cdot 4 = 24 \equiv 9 \pmod{15}\) ✓

4. Chinese Remainder Theorem

Definition 4.1 (Pairwise Coprime) A set of integers \(n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_k\) is called pairwise coprime if \(\gcd(n_i, n_j) = 1\) for all \(i \neq j\).
Theorem 4.1 (Chinese Remainder Theorem) Let \(n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_k\) be pairwise coprime positive integers. Then for any integers \(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_k\), the system of simultaneous congruences: \[\begin{cases} x \equiv a_1 \pmod{n_1} \\ x \equiv a_2 \pmod{n_2} \\ \vdots \\ x \equiv a_k \pmod{n_k} \end{cases}\] has a unique solution modulo \(N = n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k\).
Proof:
Step 1: Existence via construction

Let \(N = n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k\). For each \(i\), define \(N_i = N/n_i\) (product of all moduli except \(n_i\)).

Since \(\gcd(n_i, N_i) = 1\) (pairwise coprime), \(N_i\) has an inverse modulo \(n_i\).

Let \(M_i\) be the inverse of \(N_i\) modulo \(n_i\), so \(N_i M_i \equiv 1 \pmod{n_i}\).

Step 2: Construct the solution

Define: \[x = a_1 N_1 M_1 + a_2 N_2 M_2 + \cdots + a_k N_k M_k\]

Step 3: Verify \(x \equiv a_j \pmod{n_j}\)

Consider \(x\) modulo \(n_j\): \[x = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i N_i M_i\]

For \(i \neq j\): \(N_i\) is a multiple of \(n_j\), so \(N_i M_i \equiv 0 \pmod{n_j}\).

For \(i = j\): \(N_j M_j \equiv 1 \pmod{n_j}\), so \(a_j N_j M_j \equiv a_j \pmod{n_j}\).

Therefore \(x \equiv a_j \pmod{n_j}\).

Step 4: Prove uniqueness

Suppose \(x\) and \(x'\) are both solutions.

Then \(x \equiv x' \pmod{n_i}\) for all \(i\).

Since the \(n_i\) are pairwise coprime, \(\gcd(n_i, n_j) = 1\) for \(i \neq j\).

By the Chinese Remainder Theorem (or direct argument): if \(n_i \mid (x - x')\) for all \(i\), and the \(n_i\) are pairwise coprime, then \(N \mid (x - x')\).

Therefore \(x \equiv x' \pmod{N}\). \(\square\)

Example 4.1 Solve the system: \[\begin{cases} x \equiv 2 \pmod{3} \\ x \equiv 3 \pmod{5} \\ x \equiv 2 \pmod{7} \end{cases}\]

Step 1: Compute N and each N_i

\(N = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 105\)

\(N_1 = 105/3 = 35\), \(N_2 = 105/5 = 21\), \(N_3 = 105/7 = 15\)

Step 2: Find each M_i (inverse of N_i mod n_i)

\(M_1\): Find \(35^{-1} \pmod{3}\). Since \(35 \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\), we need \(2^{-1} \pmod{3}\):

\(2 \cdot 2 = 4 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}\), so \(M_1 = 2\).

\(M_2\): Find \(21^{-1} \pmod{5}\). Since \(21 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}\), \(M_2 = 1\).

\(M_3\): Find \(15^{-1} \pmod{7}\). Since \(15 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}\), \(M_3 = 1\).

Step 3: Compute x

\(x = 2 \cdot 35 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 21 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 15 \cdot 1\)

\(x = 140 + 63 + 30 = 233\)

\(x \equiv 233 \pmod{105}\)

\(233 = 105 \cdot 2 + 23\), so \(x \equiv 23 \pmod{105}\)

Verification:

\(23 \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\) ✓

\(23 \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\) ✓

\(23 \equiv 2 \pmod{7}\) ✓

Exercises:
  1. Prove that if \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\), then \(a^k \equiv b^k \pmod{n}\) for all \(k \geq 0\).
  2. Solve \(5x \equiv 3 \pmod{7}\).
  3. Solve \(12x \equiv 8 \pmod{20}\) and find all distinct solutions modulo 20.
  4. Use the Chinese Remainder Theorem to solve: \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{2}\), \(x \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\), \(x \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\).
  5. Prove: If \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\), then \(a^m \equiv b^m \pmod{n}\) for all \(m \geq 0\).
  6. Show that if \(n \mid a\) and \(n \mid b\), then \(n \mid (ax + by)\) for all \(x, y \in \mathbb{Z}\).
  7. Find all solutions to \(x \equiv 2 \pmod{4}\) and \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{6}\).
  8. Prove: If \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\) and \(c \equiv d \pmod{n}\), then \(ac \equiv bd \pmod{n}\).
