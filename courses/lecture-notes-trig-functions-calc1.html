Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

Calculus I - Week 6

Big picture. Trigonometric functions model periodic phenomena, and their derivatives follow from two geometric limit facts. Everything in this lecture is built from the single limit \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = 1\), together with the chain, product, and quotient rules. We derive each formula, record its domain, and then apply the calculus to slopes, inverse functions, and angular rates.

Road map and prerequisites.

  • Prerequisites: radian measure, the limit definition of the derivative, addition formulas, and the chain/product/quotient rules.
  • Two fundamental limits (radians only): \(\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1\) and \(\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} \to 0\).
  • Derivatives of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) from the definition; the other four by the quotient rule.
  • Inverse trig derivatives via implicit differentiation, with branch and \(|x|\) restrictions.

1. Radians and the Two Fundamental Limits

1.1 Why the angle must be in radians

In a circle of radius \(r\), an angle of \(\theta\) radians subtends arc length \(s = r\theta\). On the unit circle this means the arc length equals the angle itself, which is exactly what makes the sector area \(\frac{1}{2}\theta\). This geometric coincidence is the source of the clean limit \(\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1\). If \(h\) were measured in degrees, the same physical angle would be \(\frac{\pi h}{180}\) radians, and the limit of \(\frac{\sin h}{h}\) would be \(\frac{\pi}{180}\), not \(1\). Therefore:

Both limits require radians.

The statements \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = 1\) and \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} = 0\) are true only when \(h\) is measured in radians. All differentiation formulas below also assume radian measure; with degrees, an extra factor \(\frac{\pi}{180}\) would appear.

1.2 Geometric squeeze proof of \(\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1\)

Proof (unit circle, \(0 \lt h \lt \frac{\pi}{2}\)).

On the unit circle, compare three areas determined by a central angle \(h\): the inscribed triangle has area \(\frac{1}{2}\sin h\), the circular sector has area \(\frac{1}{2}h\) (this uses radians), and the circumscribed tangent triangle has area \(\frac{1}{2}\tan h\). From the picture these areas are ordered, so

\[\frac{1}{2}\sin h \lt \frac{1}{2}h \lt \frac{1}{2}\tan h.\]

Multiplying by \(2\) and dividing by \(\sin h \gt 0\) gives

\[1 \lt \frac{h}{\sin h} \lt \frac{1}{\cos h}.\]

Taking reciprocals reverses the inequalities:

\[\cos h \lt \frac{\sin h}{h} \lt 1.\]

As \(h \to 0^{+}\), \(\cos h \to 1\), so the squeeze theorem yields \(\lim_{h \to 0^{+}}\frac{\sin h}{h} = 1\). For \(h \lt 0\), write \(t = -h \gt 0\) and use \(\sin(-t) = -\sin t\), so \(\frac{\sin h}{h} = \frac{\sin t}{t}\); the quotient is an even function. Hence the two-sided limit is \(1\). ∎

1.3 Deriving the cosine limit

Proof.

Use the half-angle identity \(\cos h - 1 = -2\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)\). Then, for \(h \ne 0\),

\[\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} = \frac{-2\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{h} = -\sin\!\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)\cdot \frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{\frac{h}{2}}.\]

As \(h \to 0\), the first factor tends to \(0\) and the second tends to \(1\) by the sine limit (again in radians). Therefore \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} = 0\). ∎

Example 1.1 — Using the limits directly.

Evaluate \(\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin(3x)}{x}\) and \(\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}\).

Step 1: for the first limit, multiply and divide by \(3\): \(\frac{\sin(3x)}{x} = 3\cdot \frac{\sin(3x)}{3x} \to 3\cdot 1 = 3\).

Step 2: for the second, use \(1-\cos x = 2\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\):

\[\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}} = \frac{2\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{x^{2}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{\frac{x}{2}}\right)^{2} \to \frac{1}{2}.\]

Interpretation: near \(0\), \(\sin(3x) \approx 3x\) and \(1-\cos x \approx \frac{x^{2}}{2}\), useful approximations for later work.

Section 1 summary. Radians make arc length equal the angle on the unit circle, which yields \(\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1\) by a squeeze argument; the cosine limit follows from the half-angle identity.

2. Derivatives of Sine and Cosine

2.1 The derivative of \(\sin x\)

Proof from the definition.

Using \(\sin(x+h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h\),

\[\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin(x+h)-\sin x}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h - \sin x}{h}.\]

Group the terms:

\[= \lim_{h \to 0}\left[\sin x\cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x\cdot \frac{\sin h}{h}\right] = \sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1 = \cos x.\]

The two fundamental limits were used, so radians are essential. ∎

2.2 The derivative of \(\cos x\)

Proof from the definition.

Using \(\cos(x+h) = \cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h\),

\[\frac{d}{dx}\cos x = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h - \cos x}{h}\] \[= \lim_{h \to 0}\left[\cos x\cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} - \sin x\cdot \frac{\sin h}{h}\right] = \cos x\cdot 0 - \sin x\cdot 1 = -\sin x.\]
Example 2.1 — Chain rule with sine.

Find \(\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^{2})\).

Step 1: outside \(\sin u\), inside \(u = x^{2}\).

Step 2: outer derivative \(\cos u = \cos(x^{2})\); inner derivative \(2x\).

Step 3: \(\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^{2}) = 2x\cos(x^{2})\).

Example 2.2 — Product rule with cosine.

Find \(\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^{2}\cos x\right]\).

Step 1: \(u = x^{2}\), \(v = \cos x\), with \(u' = 2x\), \(v' = -\sin x\).

Step 2: \(u'v + uv' = 2x\cos x + x^{2}(-\sin x) = 2x\cos x - x^{2}\sin x\).

Section 2 summary. The definition plus the two limits gives \((\sin x)' = \cos x\) and \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\); composition and multiplication extend these to more complicated expressions.

3. The Remaining Four Derivatives (Quotient Rule)

3.1 Tangent

Derivation.

Write \(\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\), defined only where \(\cos x \ne 0\), that is \(x \ne \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi\) for integers \(k\). The quotient rule gives

\[\frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \frac{\cos x\cdot \cos x - \sin x\cdot (-\sin x)}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{\cos^{2}x + \sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{1}{\cos^{2}x} = \sec^{2}x.\]

Note \(\sec^{2}x \gt 0\) wherever \(\tan x\) is defined, so tangent is increasing on each branch. ∎

3.2 Cotangent

Derivation.

Write \(\cot x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), defined where \(\sin x \ne 0\), that is \(x \ne k\pi\). Then

\[\frac{d}{dx}\cot x = \frac{(-\sin x)\sin x - \cos x\cdot \cos x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\frac{\sin^{2}x+\cos^{2}x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\csc^{2}x.\]

Since \(-\csc^{2}x \lt 0\), cotangent is decreasing on each branch. ∎

3.3 Secant

Derivation.

Write \(\sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x}\), defined where \(x \ne \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi\). Differentiating the quotient \(1/\cos x\),

\[\frac{d}{dx}\sec x = \frac{0\cdot \cos x - 1\cdot (-\sin x)}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{\sin x}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \sec x\tan x.\]

3.4 Cosecant

Derivation.

Write \(\csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}\), defined where \(x \ne k\pi\). Then

\[\frac{d}{dx}\csc x = \frac{0\cdot \sin x - 1\cdot \cos x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\frac{\cos x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\csc x\cot x.\]

The six trigonometric derivatives (radians).

  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x\), all \(x\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x\), all \(x\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^{2}x\), \(x \ne \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\cot x = -\csc^{2}x\), \(x \ne k\pi\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\sec x = \sec x\tan x\), \(x \ne \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\csc x = -\csc x\cot x\), \(x \ne k\pi\).

3.5 Domain and sign table

FunctionDomainDerivativeSign of derivative
\(\sin x\)all real \(x\)\(\cos x\)positive where \(\cos x \gt 0\), negative where \(\cos x \lt 0\)

\(\cot x\)\(x \ne k\pi\)\(-\csc^{2}x\)always negative

Domain reminders.
  • Tangent and secant are undefined at odd multiples of \(\frac{\pi}{2}\); cotangent and cosecant are undefined at multiples of \(\pi\).
  • Derivatives can never be evaluated at points outside the original function's domain.
  • In quotient examples such as \(\frac{\cos x}{x}\), the additional restriction \(x \ne 0\) must be stated.

Section 3 summary. All six derivatives follow from the sine and cosine limits plus the quotient rule, and each carries a domain restriction inherited from a denominator.

4. Techniques, Patterns, and Higher Derivatives

Example 4.1 — Product rule with a trig composition.

Find \(\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^{2}\sin(x^{3})\right]\).

Step 1: \(u = x^{2}\), \(v = \sin(x^{3})\), so \(u' = 2x\) and, by the chain rule, \(v' = 3x^{2}\cos(x^{3})\).

Step 2: \(u'v + uv' = 2x\sin(x^{3}) + 3x^{4}\cos(x^{3})\).

Example 4.2 — Quotient rule with domain \(x \ne 0\).

Find \(\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\cos x}{x}\right]\) and state its domain.

Step 1 (domain): \(x \ne 0\).

Step 2 (quotient): \(\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\cos x}{x}\right] = \frac{x(-\sin x) - \cos x\cdot 1}{x^{2}} = \frac{-x\sin x - \cos x}{x^{2}}\).

Step 3 (interpret): the derivative is valid for every \(x \ne 0\); at \(x = 0\) the original function is undefined.

Example 4.3 — Chain rule with secant.

Find \(\frac{d}{dx}\sec(x^{2}+1)\).

Step 1: outside \(\sec u\), inside \(u = x^{2}+1\), with \(u' = 2x\).

Step 2: \(\frac{d}{dx}\sec(x^{2}+1) = \sec(x^{2}+1)\tan(x^{2}+1)\cdot 2x\).

4.1 Higher derivatives

The derivatives of sine and cosine repeat in a cycle of length four:

\[\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x, \quad \frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\sin x = -\sin x, \quad \frac{d^{3}}{dx^{3}}\sin x = -\cos x, \quad \frac{d^{4}}{dx^{4}}\sin x = \sin x.\]

The same four-step cycle holds for \(\cos x\), starting with \(-\sin x\). This periodicity is another expression of the fact that sine and cosine model circular motion.

Common mistakes.
  • \(\sin^{2}x\) means \((\sin x)^{2}\), with derivative \(2\sin x\cos x\); it is different from \(\sin(x^{2})\), whose derivative is \(2x\cos(x^{2})\).
  • The derivative of \(\tan x\) is \(\sec^{2}x\), not \(\tan^{2}x\); use the identity \(\sec^{2}x = 1+\tan^{2}x\) when converting.
  • Losing the minus signs in \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\), \((\cot x)' = -\csc^{2}x\), and \((\csc x)' = -\csc x\cot x\).
  • Differentiating trig functions in degrees, which would insert an extra factor \(\frac{\pi}{180}\).
Quick check 4.

Differentiate (a) \(\sin^{2}x\), (b) \(\sin(x^{2})\), (c) \(\frac{\tan x}{x}\) for \(x \ne 0\).

Answers: (a) \(2\sin x\cos x\); (b) \(2x\cos(x^{2})\); (c) \(\frac{x\sec^{2}x - \tan x}{x^{2}}\).

Section 4 summary. Combine the six basic derivatives with the product, quotient, and chain rules, always respecting domains. Sine and cosine have a four-step cycle of higher derivatives.

5. Inverse Trigonometric Functions

5.1 Strategy and branches

Inverse trig functions exist only after restricting the original function to a one-to-one branch. Implicit differentiation (or the inverse-function theorem) then produces the derivative. The chosen branch determines the sign of a square root and hence the sign of the answer.

Derivation for \(\arcsin x\).

Let \(y = \arcsin x\), so \(x = \sin y\) with \(-\frac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \frac{\pi}{2}\). Differentiating implicitly, \(1 = \cos y\,y'\), so \(y' = \frac{1}{\cos y}\). On this branch \(\cos y \ge 0\), and \(\cos y = \sqrt{1-\sin^{2}y} = \sqrt{1-x^{2}}\). Hence, for \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\),

\[\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}.\]

The endpoints \(x = \pm 1\) are excluded because the denominator vanishes there (vertical tangents). ∎

Derivation for \(\arctan x\).

Let \(y = \arctan x\), so \(x = \tan y\) with \(-\frac{\pi}{2} \lt y \lt \frac{\pi}{2}\). Then \(1 = \sec^{2}y\,y'\), so \(y' = \cos^{2}y\). Since \(\sec^{2}y = 1+\tan^{2}y = 1+x^{2}\), we obtain

\[\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \frac{1}{1+x^{2}} \quad \text{for all real } x.\]
Derivation for \(\operatorname{arcsec} x\) (the \(|x|\) condition).

Let \(y = \operatorname{arcsec} x\), so \(\sec y = x\) with \(y \in [0,\pi]\) and \(y \ne \frac{\pi}{2}\). Differentiating, \(\sec y\tan y\,y' = 1\), so \(y' = \frac{1}{x\tan y}\). Since \(\tan^{2}y = \sec^{2}y - 1 = x^{2}-1\), we have \(|\tan y| = \sqrt{x^{2}-1}\). For \(x \gt 1\) the angle lies in \([0,\frac{\pi}{2})\) and \(\tan y \gt 0\); for \(x \lt -1\) it lies in \((\frac{\pi}{2},\pi]\) and \(\tan y \lt 0\). In both cases \(x\tan y \gt 0\) and equals \(|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\). Therefore

\[\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{arcsec} x = \frac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}} \quad \text{for } |x| \gt 1.\]

The absolute value is essential: without it the derivative would be negative on the branch \(x \lt -1\), contradicting the fact that arcsec is increasing on both pieces. ∎

Derivatives of the inverse trig functions.
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\), branch \([-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]\), \(|x| \lt 1\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\arccos x = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\), branch \([0,\pi]\), \(|x| \lt 1\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\), branch \((-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})\), all \(x\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{arccot} x = -\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\), branch \((0,\pi)\), all \(x\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{arcsec} x = \frac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\), branch \([0,\pi]\) with \(y \ne \frac{\pi}{2}\), \(|x| \gt 1\).
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{arccsc} x = -\frac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\), branch \([-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]\) with \(y \ne 0\), \(|x| \gt 1\).
Example 5.1 — Chain rule with arctangent.

Find \(\frac{d}{dx}\arctan(x^{2})\).

Step 1: \(\frac{d}{du}\arctan u = \frac{1}{1+u^{2}}\), inside \(u = x^{2}\), \(u' = 2x\).

Step 2: \(\frac{d}{dx}\arctan(x^{2}) = \frac{1}{1+(x^{2})^{2}}\cdot 2x = \frac{2x}{1+x^{4}}\).

Example 5.2 — Chain rule with arcsine.

Find \(\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin(2x)\) and give its domain of validity.

Step 1: require \(|2x| \lt 1\), i.e. \(|x| \lt \frac{1}{2}\).

Step 2: \(\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin(2x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(2x)^{2}}}\cdot 2 = \frac{2}{\sqrt{1-4x^{2}}}\).

Common mistakes with inverse trig.
  • Omitting the absolute value in \(\operatorname{arcsec}\) or \(\operatorname{arccsc}\), which is wrong on the negative branch.
  • Forgetting the strict restrictions \(|x| \lt 1\) for arcsin/arccos and \(|x| \gt 1\) for arcsec/arccsc.
  • Ignoring the branch, which determines whether a square root is taken as positive or negative.

Section 5 summary. Implicit differentiation gives the inverse trig derivatives; branch choices explain the signs and the \(|x|\) conditions.

6. Critical Points of \(\sin x + \cos x\) on \([0,2\pi]\)

Example 6.1 — Find and classify all candidates.

Let \(f(x) = \sin x + \cos x\) on the closed interval \([0,2\pi]\). Find stationary points, check the endpoints, and classify them.

Step 1 (derivative): \(f'(x) = \cos x - \sin x\), which exists for all \(x\), so there are no nondifferentiable critical numbers.

Step 2 (solve \(f'=0\) carefully): \(\cos x - \sin x = 0\) gives \(\sin x = \cos x\). On \([0,2\pi]\) this means \(x = \frac{\pi}{4}\) or \(x = \frac{5\pi}{4}\). If instead we divide by \(\cos x\) to write \(\tan x = 1\), we must check where \(\cos x = 0\) separately: at \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\), \(f' = 0 - 1 = -1 \ne 0\); at \(x = \frac{3\pi}{2}\), \(f' = 0 - (-1) = 1 \ne 0\). So no solutions are lost, and neither of these is stationary.

Step 3 (include endpoints): the closed-interval candidates are \(x = 0, \frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{5\pi}{4}, 2\pi\).

Step 4 (evaluate and classify): \(f(0) = 1\), \(f\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sqrt{2}\), \(f\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{4}\right) = -\sqrt{2}\), \(f(2\pi) = 1\).

Step 5 (interpret): \(x = \frac{\pi}{4}\) is the absolute maximum with value \(\sqrt{2}\); \(x = \frac{5\pi}{4}\) is the absolute minimum with value \(-\sqrt{2}\). The endpoints \(x = 0\) and \(x = 2\pi\) give \(1\), which is neither an absolute maximum nor minimum. Equivalently, \(\sin x + \cos x = \sqrt{2}\sin\!\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\), confirming amplitude \(\sqrt{2}\).

Section 6 summary. When solving \(f'(x) = 0\), verify any values where a division step would be invalid (such as \(\cos x = 0\)), and always evaluate endpoints on a closed interval before classifying.

7. An Angular Related-Rates Example

Example 7.1 — The angle of a sliding ladder.

A 10 ft ladder leans against a wall. The bottom slides away at \(\frac{dx}{dt} = 2\) ft/s. At the instant \(x = 6\) ft and \(y = 8\) ft, find how fast the angle \(\theta\) between the ladder and the ground is changing.

Step 1 (relate the angle): by right-triangle trigonometry, \(\tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}\), where \(x\) and \(y\) both depend on time.

Step 2 (differentiate with respect to \(t\)): \(\sec^{2}\theta\,\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{x\frac{dy}{dt} - y\frac{dx}{dt}}{x^{2}}\).

Step 3 (find \(\frac{dy}{dt}\)): from \(x^{2}+y^{2} = 100\), \(2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0\), so \(2(6)(2) + 2(8)\frac{dy}{dt} = 0\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{3}{2}\) ft/s.

Step 4 (evaluate): \(\tan\theta = \frac{8}{6} = \frac{4}{3}\), so \(\sec^{2}\theta = 1+\tan^{2}\theta = \frac{25}{9}\). The right side is \(\frac{6\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right) - 8(2)}{36} = \frac{-9-16}{36} = -\frac{25}{36}\).

Step 5 (solve): \(\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{-25/36}{25/9} = -\frac{1}{4}\) rad/s. The negative sign means the angle is decreasing; the ladder is flattening at \(\frac{1}{4}\) radian per second.

Interpretation and units.

Angular rates are measured in radians per unit time, never degrees per second, because the differentiation formulas require radians. Differentiate before substituting the numerical values of \(x\) and \(y\).

Section 7 summary. For angular rates, write a trig relation such as \(\tan\theta = y/x\), differentiate with respect to time, and use the other known rates to solve for \(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\) in radians per unit time.

8. Bonus: Why Radians Are the Natural Measure

Bonus — The uniqueness of radians.

Let \(h\) denote an angle in some unit, and suppose \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = L\). The derivative of \(\sin x\) would then be \(L\cos x\). Setting \(L = 1\) makes the derivative of sine equal cosine exactly, and makes the derivative of cosine equal \(-\sin x\). Radians are precisely the unit for which \(L = 1\), equivalently the unit satisfying arc length \(s = r\theta\) and sector area \(\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\theta\). In degrees, \(L = \frac{\pi}{180}\), and every trig derivative would carry that extra factor. Calculus is therefore simplest, and the formulas above are valid as written, only in radians.

10. Overall Summary

Radian measure gives \(\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1\) by a squeeze argument and \(\frac{\cos h-1}{h} \to 0\) by the half-angle identity. These yield \((\sin x)' = \cos x\) and \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\); the quotient rule gives the other four derivatives and their domain restrictions. Inverse trig derivatives follow from implicit differentiation, with branch choices producing the \(|x|\) conditions for arcsec and arccsc. Sine and cosine derivatives cycle every four steps, and trig relations such as \(\tan\theta = y/x\) turn geometric situations into angular-rate calculations measured in radians per unit time.
