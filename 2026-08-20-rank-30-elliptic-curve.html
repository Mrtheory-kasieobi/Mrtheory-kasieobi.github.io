A Rank-30 Elliptic Curve Surpasses the Previous Record

August 21, 2026 ~5 min read Elliptic Curves

When I checked the ICARM leaderboard this morning, entry #273 had just appeared, reporting a new record: an elliptic curve with rank at least 30. The curve was submitted yesterday by Claude, Levent Alpöge, and Ava Howell. What struck me immediately was that this pushes the record up from 29, set by Elkies and collaborators in 2024, to exactly 30, all in just two years.

I find myself thinking about what "rank 30" actually means here. Most people know elliptic curves through their canonical form $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$, but this particular curve is given by a more general equation:

$$y^2 + xy = x^3 + ax + b$$

with specific coefficients $a = -201769035260418549083594900060734240952308696994802735114305555$ and $b = 1151107939141058565733479426024323225135665982951300586808823640527729578307228357301072889377$. These enormous numbers are why the curve carries so many records simultaneously.

Elliptic Curves and Their Groups

An elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ can be written in Weierstrass form. While the simple equation $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ is common, the general form allows linear terms and coefficients in any characteristic. The key is that the discriminant $\Delta \neq 0$, ensuring the curve is nonsingular.

The set of rational points $E(\mathbb{Q})$ forms an abelian group under the chord-and-tangent construction. The Mordell–Weil theorem tells us this group decomposes canonically as

$$E(\mathbb{Q}) \cong E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}} \oplus \mathbb{Z}^r$$

The integer $r$ is the rank. It measures how many independent directions the rational points span, how many generators the free part has.

What "Rank at Least 30" Actually Means

The ICARM record shows $\operatorname{rank} E(\mathbb{Q}) \ge 30$. This is a lower bound, not an exact value. To establish it, the submitters had to demonstrate 30 independent rational points on the curve.

Independence in this context means that in the Mordell–Weil group, no nontrivial integer linear combination of these points equals the identity $\mathcal{O}$. Formally, points $P_1, \ldots, P_{30}$ are independent if

$$n_1 P_1 + n_2 P_2 + \cdots + n_{30} P_{30} = \mathcal{O}$$

implies all $n_i = 0$ (up to torsion). This is not ordinary linear independence, it's independence in a finitely generated abelian group.

Why 30 Matters for the Record

Finding 30 independent points is substantial. The curve comes with 30 witness points explicitly listed, each with enormous coordinates, and their independence has been certified. The ICARM leaderboard only accepts entries with such computational certificates, so these records are rigorously verified, not just reported.

The curve also has the smallest discriminant among all rank-30 curves, and holds records for naive height, Faltings height, and discriminant. That's why it's listed as a record in multiple categories.

The Historical Progression

The chain of record progression is remarkable:

  • 2006: Elkies (with Sutherland) establishes rank $\ge 28$
  • 2024: Elkies and collaborators push to rank $\ge 29$
  • August 2026: Alpöge, Howell, and Claude reach rank $\ge 30$

The rank-28 record held for 18 years. That each subsequent increment required new techniques shows how challenging this problem is, even with increasing computational power.

Under GRH and BSD, the Rank Is Exactly 30

The ICARM commentary notes that assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis and the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, the rank is exactly 30. Under these assumptions, the analytic rank (the order of vanishing of $L(E,s)$ at $s=1$) can be bounded, and combined with the trivial rank bound, gives an exact value. This is conditional, but provides strong evidence that 30 is the true rank.

Update - August 23, 2026

Since this article was published, a new elliptic curve with rank at least 31 has been reported/submitted, establishing a new record. See the follow-up article on the rank-31 curve for the latest developments.

The Final Question

Are elliptic curve ranks bounded over $\mathbb{Q}$? We don't know. Each new record simply raises the known lower bound. The existence of rank-30 curves shows the rank can be at least 30, but it doesn't prove unboundedness. Some number theorists expect ranks to be unbounded; others suspect there's a maximum. This discovery keeps both possibilities very much alive.
