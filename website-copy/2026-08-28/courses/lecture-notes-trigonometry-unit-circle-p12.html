Trigonometry and the Unit Circle

Precalculus 12 - Lecture Notes
British Columbia Curriculum | Last Updated: 2026

1. Angles and Angle Measure

1.1 Why Study Angles?

Angles are fundamental to understanding periodic phenomena in nature - from the rotation of planets to the oscillation of waves. The study of angles connects geometry with algebra through the unit circle, providing a bridge to more advanced mathematics.

1.2 Angle Measurement Systems

Angles can be measured in degrees or radians. The radian measure is based on the radius of a circle, making it the natural choice for higher mathematics.

Why Radians?

Radians are preferred in calculus and higher mathematics because they create a direct relationship between arc length and angle measure. When an angle is measured in radians, the arc length equals the radius times the angle: s = rθ.

Conversion Formulas:
\[180° = \pi \text{ radians}\] \[\text{radians} = \text{degrees} \times \frac{\pi}{180}\] \[\text{degrees} = \text{radians} \times \frac{180}{\pi}\]
Example 1: Converting Between Units

Convert \(120°\) to radians.

\[120° \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{2\pi}{3} \text{ radians}\]

Motivation: This conversion is essential for working with trigonometric functions in calculus, where all formulas assume radian measure.

1.3 Arc Length and Sector Area

For a circle of radius \(r\) and central angle \(\theta\) in radians, we can calculate the arc length and sector area.

Arc Length: \(s = r\theta\)
Sector Area: \(A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta\)
Example 2: Real-World Application

A Ferris wheel has radius 20 meters. If it completes one full revolution in 4 minutes, what is the angular speed in radians per minute?

One revolution = \(2\pi\) radians, so angular speed = \(\frac{2\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}\) rad/min

Motivation: Understanding angular speed helps us model periodic motion in physics and engineering.

2. The Unit Circle

2.1 Definition and Properties

The unit circle is a circle with radius 1 centered at the origin. This simple geometric object is the foundation for all trigonometric functions and connects right triangle trigonometry to the coordinate plane.

Unit Circle Definition: For any angle \(\theta\) in standard position (vertex at origin, initial side on positive x-axis), the point where the terminal arm intersects the unit circle has coordinates \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\).

2.2 Why the Unit Circle Matters

The unit circle extends trigonometry beyond acute angles. While right triangle definitions only work for angles between 0° and 90°, the unit circle allows us to define trigonometric functions for any real number.

Example 3: Understanding the Unit Circle

Consider angle \(\theta = \frac{5\pi}{6}\) (150°). This angle is in the second quadrant, where x-coordinates are negative and y-coordinates are positive. Therefore, \(\cos\theta < 0\) and \(\sin\theta > 0\).

Motivation: This explains why cosine is negative and sine is positive for obtuse angles - a key insight for understanding trigonometric signs in different quadrants.

2.3 Special Angles and Symmetry

Key angles and their trigonometric values on the unit circle. These values are derived from special right triangles (30-60-90 and 45-45-90).

Angle (rad) Angle (°) \(\cos\theta\) \(\sin\theta\) \(\tan\theta\)
0 0° 1 0 0
\(\pi/6\) 30° \(\sqrt{3}/2\) 1/2 \(\sqrt{3}/3\)
\(\pi/4\) 45° \(\sqrt{2}/2\) \(\sqrt{2}/2\) 1
\(\pi/3\) 60° 1/2 \(\sqrt{3}/2\) \(\sqrt{3}\)
\(\pi/2\) 90° 0 1 undefined

2.4 Reference Angles

For any angle, we can find its reference angle - the acute angle to the x-axis. This helps us determine trigonometric values in all quadrants.

Reference Angle Rules:
  • Quadrant I: Reference angle = \(\theta\)
  • Quadrant II: Reference angle = \(\pi - \theta\)
  • Quadrant III: Reference angle = \(\theta - \pi\)
  • Quadrant IV: Reference angle = \(2\pi - \theta\)

3. Trigonometric Ratios

3.1 Definitions on the Unit Circle

For any angle \(\theta\) in standard position, the six trigonometric functions are defined as ratios of the coordinates on the unit circle.

\[\sin\theta = y\text{-coordinate of point on unit circle}\] \[\cos\theta = x\text{-coordinate of point on unit circle}\] \[\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{y}{x}}\]
Example 4: Finding Trig Values

Find \(\sin\frac{3\pi}{4}\), \(\cos\frac{3\pi}{4}\), and \(\tan\frac{3\pi}{4}\).

The angle \(\frac{3\pi}{4}\) is in quadrant II, with reference angle \(\frac{\pi}{4}\).

\(\sin\frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\) (positive in QII)

\(\cos\frac{3\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\) (negative in QII)

\(\tan\frac{3\pi}{4} = -1\) (negative in QII)

Motivation: Understanding how signs change by quadrant is crucial for solving trigonometric equations and analyzing wave behavior.

3.2 Reciprocal and Quotient Identities

The remaining four trigonometric functions are defined as reciprocals of the primary functions. These relationships are essential for simplifying complex trigonometric expressions.

\[\csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}, \quad \sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}, \quad \cot\theta = \frac{1}{\tan\theta} = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\]

3.3 Signs of Trigonometric Functions

The signs of trigonometric functions depend on the quadrant. This is summarized by the acronym "All Students Take Calculus":

Quadrant \(\sin\theta\) \(\cos\theta\) \(\tan\theta\)
I + + +
II + - -
III - - +
IV - + -

4. Introduction to Trigonometric Equations

4.1 Solving Basic Trigonometric Equations

To solve equations like \(\sin\theta = a\) or \(\cos\theta = a\), we use the unit circle to find all solutions. This process connects algebraic problem-solving with geometric intuition.

Example 5: Solving Trigonometric Equations

Solve \(\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}\) for \(\theta \in [0, 2\pi)\).

From the unit circle, \(\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}\) at \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}\) and \(\theta = \frac{5\pi}{6}\).

Motivation: These solutions represent the two angles in the first and second quadrants where the y-coordinate equals 1/2. This geometric interpretation helps us understand wave interference and signal processing.

4.2 General Solutions

Trigonometric equations have infinitely many solutions due to the periodic nature of trigonometric functions. We express them using the period.

General Solution for Sine:
\[\sin\theta = a \Rightarrow \theta = \arcsin(a) + 2\pi n \text{ or } \theta = \pi - \arcsin(a) + 2\pi n\]

General Solution for Cosine:
\[\cos\theta = a \Rightarrow \theta = \pm\arccos(a) + 2\pi n\]
Example 6: General Solutions

Find all solutions to \(\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}\).

Principal solutions: \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{5\pi}{6}\)

General solutions: \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{6} + 2\pi n\) or \(\theta = \frac{5\pi}{6} + 2\pi n\) for any integer \(n\)

Motivation: This infinite family of solutions models periodic phenomena like the times when a pendulum reaches a certain height.

5. Applications of Trigonometry

5.1 Real-World Applications

Trigonometry is essential for modeling periodic phenomena. From the height of tides to the voltage in alternating current, trigonometric functions describe oscillations in nature and technology.

Example 7: Modeling with Trigonometry

A Ferris wheel with radius 25 meters completes one revolution every 4 minutes. If you board at the bottom, what is your height after 90 seconds?

Angular position: \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} \times \frac{90}{60} = \frac{3\pi}{4}\) radians

Height: \(h = 25 + 25\sin\frac{3\pi}{4} = 25 + 25 \times \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 39.3\) meters

Motivation: This demonstrates how trigonometry models circular motion, which is fundamental to understanding waves, rotations, and oscillations.

6. Practice Problems

Problem 1

Convert \(\frac{5\pi}{6}\) radians to degrees and find the reference angle.

Problem 2

Find all six trigonometric values for \(\theta = \frac{7\pi}{4}\) and explain the signs in each quadrant.

Problem 3

Solve \(\cos\theta = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) for \(\theta \in [0, 2\pi)\) and give the general solution.

Problem 4

A lighthouse is located on a small island 2 km from the nearest point P on a straight shoreline. The light rotates at 5 revolutions per minute. Find the rate at which the beam moves along the shore when it is 1 km from point P.
