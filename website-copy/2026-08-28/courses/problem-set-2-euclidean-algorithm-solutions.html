Solutions: Problem Set 2: Euclidean Algorithm and Bézout's Identity

Elementary Number Theory Medium

Solutions

Problem 1

Solution: Euclidean algorithm: \(1071 = 462 \cdot 2 + 147\), \(462 = 147 \cdot 3 + 21\), \(147 = 21 \cdot 7 + 0\). So \(\gcd(1071, 462) = 21\).

Problem 2

Solution: Extended Euclidean algorithm: \(56 = 15 \cdot 3 + 11\), \(15 = 11 \cdot 1 + 4\), \(11 = 4 \cdot 2 + 3\), \(4 = 3 \cdot 1 + 1\). Back-substituting: \(1 = 4 - 3 = 4 - (11 - 4 \cdot 2) = 3 \cdot 4 - 11 = 3(15 - 11) - 11 = 3 \cdot 15 - 4 \cdot 11 = 3 \cdot 15 - 4(56 - 15 \cdot 3) = 15 \cdot 15 - 56 \cdot 4\). So \(x = 15, y = -4\).

Problem 3

Solution: \(120 = 23 \cdot 5 + 5\), \(23 = 5 \cdot 4 + 3\), \(5 = 3 \cdot 1 + 2\), \(3 = 2 \cdot 1 + 1\), \(2 = 1 \cdot 2 + 0\). GCD is 1. Back-substitution gives the linear combination.

Problem 4

Solution: Bezout's identity states that \(\gcd(a, b)\) is the smallest positive integer of the form \(ax + by\).

Problem 5

Solution: \(\gcd(a+b, a-b) = \gcd(a+b, (a+b) - (a-b)) = \gcd(a+b, 2b) = \gcd(a+b, 2)\) when \(a, b\) are odd.

Problem 6

Solution: Since \(\gcd(15, 25) = 5\) and \(5 \mid 5\), solutions exist. One solution: \(x = 2, y = -1\) since \(15 \cdot 2 + 25 \cdot (-1) = 5\). General: \(x = 2 + 5t, y = -1 - 3t\).

Problem 7

Solution: If \(ax + by = c\) has solutions, then \(c = ax + by\). Any common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\) divides \(ax + by = c\).

Problem 8

Solution: Using extended Euclidean algorithm: \(31 = 17 \cdot 1 + 14\), \(17 = 14 \cdot 1 + 3\), \(14 = 3 \cdot 4 + 2\), \(3 = 2 \cdot 1 + 1\). So \(1 = 3 - 2 = 3 - (14 - 3 \cdot 4) = 5 \cdot 3 - 14 = 5(17 - 14) - 14 = 5 \cdot 17 - 6 \cdot 14 = 5 \cdot 17 - 6(31 - 17) = 7 \cdot 17 - 6 \cdot 31\). So \(17^{-1} \equiv 7 \pmod{31}\).

Solution: \(81 = 36 \cdot 2 + 9\), \(36 = 9 \cdot 4 + 0\). So \(\gcd(81, 36) = 9\).

Bonus Problems

Bonus Problem 1

Solution:

Step 1: Let \(S = \{ax + by : x, y \in \mathbb{Z}, ax + by > 0\}\). This set is non-empty since \(\gcd(a, b) \in S\).

Step 2: Let \(d\) be the smallest element of \(S\). We want to show \(d = \gcd(a, b)\).

Step 3: By the division algorithm, for any \(n \in S\), write \(n = qd + r\) where \(0 \leq r < d\).

Step 4: Since \(n = ax + by\) and \(d = ax_0 + by_0\), we have \(r = n - qd = a(x - qx_0) + b(y - qy_0)\), so \(r \in S\) or \(r = 0\).

Step 5: Since \(d\) is minimal in \(S\) and \(0 \leq r < d\), we must have \(r = 0\). Thus \(d \mid n\) for all \(n \in S\).

Step 6: In particular, \(d \mid a\) and \(d \mid b\) (since \(a, b \in S\) when \(\gcd(a, b) \neq 0\)).

Step 7: Since \(d \in S\), \(d = ax_0 + by_0\) for some \(x_0, y_0\). Thus any common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\) divides \(d\).

Conclusion: Therefore, \(d = \gcd(a, b)\).

Bonus Problem 2

Solution:

Step 1: Assume \(\sqrt{p} = \frac{a}{b}\) in lowest terms, where \(p\) is prime.

Step 2: Then \(p b^2 = a^2\). Since \(p\) is prime and \(p \mid a^2\), we have \(p \mid a\) by Euclid's lemma.

Step 3: Write \(a = pc\). Substituting: \(p b^2 = p^2 c^2\), so \(b^2 = pc^2\).

Step 4: Since \(p \mid b^2\) and \(p\) is prime, \(p \mid b\).

Step 5: We have \(p \mid a\) and \(p \mid b\), contradicting \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\).

Conclusion: Therefore, \(\sqrt{p}\) is irrational.

Bonus Problem 3

Solution:

Step 1: Let \(\gcd(a, b) = d\). Then \(a = da'\) and \(b = db'\) for some integers \(a', b'\).

Step 2: We have \(\gcd(a', b') = 1\) since any common divisor of \(a'\) and \(b'\) would multiply \(d\) to give a larger common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\).

Step 3: If \(\gcd(a', b') = k > 1\), then \(kd \mid a\) and \(kd \mid b\), contradicting \(d = \gcd(a, b)\).

Conclusion: Therefore, \(\gcd\left(\frac{a}{d}, \frac{b}{d}\right) = 1\).
