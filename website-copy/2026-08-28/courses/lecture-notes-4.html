Lecture 4: Linear Congruences and the Chinese Remainder Theorem

Lecture 4Linear Congruences and the Chinese Remainder Theorem

1. Linear Congruences

Definition 4.1 (Linear Congruence) A linear congruence is an equation of the form \[ax \equiv b \pmod{n}\] where \(a, b, n \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(n > 0\).
Example 4.1 Solve the linear congruence \(3x \equiv 1 \pmod{7}\).

We need to find \(x\) such that \(3x - 1\) is divisible by 7.

Testing values: \(x = 5\) gives \(3 \cdot 5 = 15 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}\). So \(x \equiv 5 \pmod{7}\) is the solution.

Verification: \(3 \cdot 5 = 15 = 7 \cdot 2 + 1\), so \(15 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}\). \(\square\)

Theorem 4.1 The linear congruence \(ax \equiv b \pmod{n}\) has a solution if and only if \(\gcd(a, n) \mid b\).
Proof:

Necessity (\(\Rightarrow\)): Suppose \(x_0\) is a solution, so \(ax_0 \equiv b \pmod{n}\). Then \(n \mid (ax_0 - b)\), so \(ax_0 - b = nk\) for some integer \(k\).

Rewriting: \(ax_0 - nk = b\). Any common divisor of \(a\) and \(n\) divides the left side, hence divides \(b\). Thus \(\gcd(a, n) \mid b\). \(\square\)

Sufficiency (\(\Leftarrow\)): Assume \(\gcd(a, n) \mid b\). Write \(d = \gcd(a, n)\) and \(b = dk\).

By Bézout's Identity, there exist integers \(u, v\) such that \(au + nv = d\).

Multiplying by \(k\): \(a(uk) + n(vk) = dk = b\). Thus \(a(uk) \equiv b \pmod{n}\), so \(x = uk\) is a solution. \(\square\)

2. Number of Solutions to Linear Congruences

Theorem 4.2 If the linear congruence \(ax \equiv b \pmod{n}\) has a solution, then it has exactly \(\frac{n}{\gcd(a, n)}\) incongruent solutions modulo \(n\).
Proof:

Let \(d = \gcd(a, n)\). By Theorem 4.1, \(d \mid b\), so a solution exists.

Let \(x_0\) be a particular solution. We claim all solutions are of the form \(x = x_0 + \frac{n}{d} \cdot k\) for \(k = 0, 1, \ldots, d-1\).

Verification:

If \(x = x_0 + \frac{n}{d} \cdot k\), then: \[ax = a\left(x_0 + \frac{n}{d} k\right) = ax_0 + \frac{an}{d} k\] Since \(d = \gcd(a, n)\), we have \(\frac{n}{d}\) is an integer. Also, \(n \mid \frac{an}{d} k\) since \(n \mid an\).

Thus \(ax \equiv ax_0 \equiv b \pmod{n}\). \(\square\)

3. Chinese Remainder Theorem

Definition 4.2 (Pairwise Coprime) Integers \(m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_k\) are pairwise coprime if \(\gcd(m_i, m_j) = 1\) for all \(i \neq j\).
Theorem 4.3 (Chinese Remainder Theorem) Let \(m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_k\) be pairwise coprime positive integers. Then for any integers \(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_k\), there exists an integer \(x\) such that: \[x \equiv a_i \pmod{m_i} \quad \text{for all } i = 1, 2, \ldots, k.\] Moreover, this solution is unique modulo \(M = m_1 m_2 \cdots m_k\).
Proof:

Existence (by induction on \(k\)):

Base case (\(k = 2\)): Let \(m_1, m_2\) be coprime. We want \(x\) such that \(x \equiv a_1 \pmod{m_1}\) and \(x \equiv a_2 \pmod{m_2}\).

By Bézout's Identity, there exist integers \(u, v\) with \(um_1 + vm_2 = 1\).

Set \(x = a_1 \cdot vm_2 + a_2 \cdot um_1\).

Then \(x \equiv a_1 vm_2 \equiv a_1 \cdot 1 \equiv a_1 \pmod{m_1}\) (since \(vm_2 \equiv 1 \pmod{m_1}\)).

Similarly, \(x \equiv a_2 \pmod{m_2}\). \(\square\)

Inductive step: Assume the theorem holds for \(k-1\) congruences.

By the base case, there exists \(y\) such that \(y \equiv a_i \pmod{m_i}\) for \(i = 1, \ldots, k-1\) and \(y \equiv a_k \pmod{m_k}\).

By the induction hypothesis, such a \(y\) exists. \(\square\)

4. Applications of CRT

Example 4.2 Solve the system: \[x \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\] \[x \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\] \[x \equiv 2 \pmod{7}\]

Solution:

Step 1: Solve the first two congruences. We need \(x\) such that \(x \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\) and \(x \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\).

By Bézout's Identity: \(2 \cdot 5 + (-3) \cdot 3 = 1\), so \(u = 2, v = -3\).

Using CRT formula: \(x = 2 \cdot (-3) \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 = -18 + 30 = 12\).

Check: \(12 \equiv 0 \pmod{3}\)... Wait, let me recalculate.

Actually: \(x = a_1 \cdot v_1 \cdot m_2 + a_2 \cdot u_1 \cdot m_1\) where \(u_1 m_1 + v_1 m_2 = 1\).

With \(2 \cdot 5 + (-3) \cdot 3 = 1\): \(x = 2 \cdot (-3) \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 = -18 + 30 = 12\).

Check: \(12 \equiv 0 \pmod{3}\)... Error! Let me use: \(x = a_1 + m_1 \cdot k\) where \(a_1 + m_1 k \equiv a_2 \pmod{m_2}\).

We need \(2 + 3k \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\), so \(3k \equiv 1 \pmod{5}\), giving \(k \equiv 2 \pmod{5}\).

Thus \(x = 2 + 3 \cdot 2 = 8\). Check: \(8 \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\) ✓

Now solve: \(x \equiv 8 \pmod{15}\) and \(x \equiv 2 \pmod{7}\).

We need \(8 + 15k \equiv 2 \pmod{7}\), so \(1 + k \equiv 2 \pmod{7}\), giving \(k \equiv 1 \pmod{7}\).

Thus \(x = 8 + 15 \cdot 1 = 23\). \(\square\)

Exercises:
  1. Solve \(4x \equiv 2 \pmod{6}\). How many incongruent solutions are there?
  2. Solve \(5x \equiv 3 \pmod{7}\).
  3. Solve the system: \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{2}\), \(x \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\), \(x \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\).
  4. Prove: If \(m \mid n\) and \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\), then \(a \equiv b \pmod{m}\).
  5. Find all solutions to \(7x \equiv 5 \pmod{12}\).
  6. Solve: \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\), \(x \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\), \(x \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\).
  7. Show that if \(\gcd(a, n) = 1\), then the congruence \(ax \equiv b \pmod{n}\) has a unique solution modulo \(n\).
