Lecture 1: Measurement

Mathematics 10 - Chapter 1

1.1 Imperial Measures of Length

The Imperial system uses inches, feet, yards, and miles for measuring length. Understanding these units is essential for working with measurements in everyday life and in some technical fields.

Why Study Imperial Units? While most of the world uses metric, the US and some industries still use Imperial measurements. Construction, real estate, and aviation often use feet and inches. Being able to convert between systems is a practical life skill.

Imperial Length Conversions

Key Conversion Factors:
1 foot = 12 inches
1 yard = 3 feet = 36 inches
1 mile = 1760 yards = 5280 feet

Example 1: Converting Feet to Inches

A room is 15 feet long. How many inches is this?

15 feet × 12 inches/foot = 180 inches

Motivation: This conversion is needed when buying flooring or curtains that are sold by the inch.

1.2 Math Lab: Measuring Length and Distance

In this lab, you'll practice measuring objects using rulers, tape measures, and other tools. You'll learn to:

  • Read measurements to the nearest fraction of an inch
  • Use significant figures in measurements
  • Estimate measurements before taking them
  • Understand measurement uncertainty and precision

Real-World Connection: Carpenters, engineers, and scientists all need to measure accurately. A small error in measurement can lead to a bridge that doesn't fit or a machine that doesn't work.

1.3 Relating SI and Imperial Units

The SI (metric) system uses meters, centimeters, and kilometers. To convert between systems, we use the exact relationship: 1 inch = 2.54 cm.

Conversion Factor

To convert from inches to centimeters: multiply by 2.54
To convert from centimeters to inches: divide by 2.54
To convert from feet to meters: multiply by 0.3048
To convert from meters to feet: divide by 0.3048

Example 2: Converting Meters to Feet

A room is 4.5 meters wide. What is this in feet?

4.5 meters ÷ 0.3048 meters/foot ≈ 14.76 feet

Motivation: This is useful when traveling to countries that use different measurement systems or when working with international specifications.

1.4 Surface Areas of Right Pyramids and Right Cones

A right pyramid has a base that is a regular polygon and the apex is directly above the center. A right cone has a circular base and the apex is directly above the center.

Why These Shapes Matter: Pyramids and cones appear in architecture, packaging, and nature. The Louvre Pyramid, traffic cones, and even some mountains have these shapes. Understanding their surface area helps us calculate materials needed for construction or painting.

Surface Area Formulas

For a right pyramid with a regular polygonal base: \(SA = B + \frac{1}{2}Ps\)

where B is the base area, P is the perimeter, and s is the slant height.

For a right cone: \(SA = \pi r^2 + \pi r l\)

where r is the radius and l is the slant height.

1.5 Volumes of Right Pyramids and Right Cones

The volume of a right pyramid or cone is one-third the volume of a prism or cylinder with the same base and height.

Why One-Third? This relationship can be understood by filling a cylinder with water and pouring it into three identical pyramids with the same base and height. This principle is fundamental to calculus and was discovered by ancient mathematicians.

Volume Formulas

For a right pyramid: \(V = \frac{1}{3}Bh\) where B is base area and h is height.

For a right cone: \(V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h\)

Example 3: Volume of a Traffic Cone

A traffic cone has radius 10 cm and height 35 cm. What is its volume?

\(V = \frac{1}{3}\pi(10)^2(35) = \frac{3500\pi}{3} \approx 3665 \text{ cm}^3\)

Motivation: This helps manufacturers determine how much plastic is needed to make each cone.

1.6 Surface Area and Volume of a Sphere

A sphere is a three-dimensional shape where all points on the surface are equidistant from the center. This distance is called the radius.

Why Spheres Are Special: Spheres have the smallest surface area for a given volume, which is why soap bubbles and water droplets form spherical shapes. This principle is used in engineering to minimize material usage.

Sphere Formulas

Formulas for a sphere with radius r:

Surface Area: \(SA = 4\pi r^2\)

Volume: \(V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\)

Example 4: Volume of a Basketball

A basketball has radius 12 cm. What is its volume and surface area?

\(V = \frac{4}{3}\pi(12)^3 = 724 \text{ cm}^3\)

\(SA = 4\pi(12)^2 = 572 \text{ cm}^2\)

Motivation: Manufacturers use these calculations to ensure basketballs meet official size requirements.

1.7 Solving Problems Involving Objects

When solving measurement problems, follow this systematic approach:

  1. Identify the shape and what you need to find
  2. Determine the appropriate formula
  3. Ensure all measurements are in the same unit
  4. Substitute values and calculate
  5. Check if your answer is reasonable

Real-World Applications: These skills are used in construction, manufacturing, cooking, and many other fields where precise measurements matter.
