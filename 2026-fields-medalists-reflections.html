The 2026 Fields Medal: Before and after the Official Announcement

July 14-23, 2026 ~5 min read Research Reflections

Every four years, mathematicians from around the world gather for the International Congress of Mathematicians (ICM). Alongside lectures, research talks, and the countless conversations that take place during the congress, there is one moment that attracts attention far beyond the mathematical community: the announcement of the Fields Medalists.

The Fields Medal is one of the most famous prizes in mathematics. It is awarded every four years to mathematicians who have made outstanding contributions to their field and who were under the age of forty on January 1 of the award year. The medal was first awarded in 1936, following the vision of Canadian mathematician John Charles Fields. Unlike a prize intended simply to recognize a lifetime of work, the Fields Medal was designed to recognize major achievements while also encouraging mathematicians who still have much of their careers ahead of them.

Normally, the names of the recipients remain secret until the opening ceremony.

In 2026, that did not quite happen.

Waiting for July 23

By July 14, the mathematical community was just over a week away from the opening of ICM 2026 in Philadelphia. The Fields Medal Committee had made its decisions, but officially, nobody outside the relevant circles was supposed to know who had been selected.

There was, of course, plenty of speculation.

Every Fields Medal year produces predictions, discussions, arguments, and long lists of mathematicians whose work might be recognized. Mathematicians and students debate which problems have been solved, which areas of mathematics are becoming especially influential, and whether a particular result is "Fields Medal-worthy." Much of this is informal, and much of it is simply part of the fun of following mathematics.

The Leak

On July 13 and 14, people examining the data behind the official ICM 2026 website noticed several schedule entries that were not supposed to be visible. Among them were entries labelled "HIDDEN Fields Medal Lecture." The names attached to those entries were:

  • Yu Deng
  • John Pardon
  • Jacob Tsimerman
  • Hong Wang

The information did not appear as part of the normal public schedule. Instead, it could be found in data being sent to the website. Once people realized what the entries appeared to represent, the discovery spread quickly through Reddit and other online communities.

At that point, there was an unusual situation: the official announcement had not happened, but the identities of the apparent recipients were already circulating widely.

Of course, there was still a reason to be cautious. A hidden entry on a website is not, by itself, the same thing as an official announcement from the International Mathematical Union. Until the ceremony took place, the names were technically unconfirmed.

The Four Names

The four names were not random guesses. Each of the four mathematicians had already made substantial contributions to their respective areas of mathematics.

Yu Deng works in partial differential equations, kinetic theory, and mathematical physics.

John Pardon works in symplectic geometry, topology, and related areas of geometry.

Jacob Tsimerman works in arithmetic and complex algebraic geometry.

Hong Wang works in harmonic analysis and geometric measure theory.

The mathematical community was left in the strange position of discussing the apparent recipients while simultaneously waiting to find out whether the apparent recipients were actually the recipients.

July 23

That question was finally answered on July 23.

During the opening ceremony of ICM 2026, the International Mathematical Union officially announced Yu Deng, John Pardon, Jacob Tsimerman, and Hong Wang as the 2026 Fields Medalists. The four names revealed through the leaked website data were, in fact, the four names announced at the ceremony.

So, in retrospect, the leak was completely accurate.

Yu Deng - "For his work in partial differential equations, including the rigorous derivation of the Boltzmann equation from hard-sphere dynamics for rarefied gases, the derivation of wave kinetic equations from nonlinear dispersive systems, and probabilistic approaches to nonlinear Schrodinger dynamics."

John Pardon - "For his achievements in symplectic geometry including new approaches to virtual fundamental cycles, Fukaya categories of certain manifolds and counting holomorphic curves, and for his contributions to other areas of geometry and topology, including group actions on 3-manifolds and knot theory."

Jacob Tsimerman - "For his contribution in the recasting of o-minimality as a fundamental method of arithmetic and complex algebraic geometry, and his role in the proof of many central conjectures including Griffiths' conjecture on the algebraicity of images of the period maps, and the Andre-Oort conjecture for Siegel modular varieties."

Hong Wang - "For her work in harmonic analysis and geometric measure theory, including applications of multiscale and decoupling techniques to the local smoothing conjecture for the planar wave equation, and major advances in Fourier restriction, Falconer distance sets, Furstenberg sets in the plane, and the Kakeya problem in three dimensions."
